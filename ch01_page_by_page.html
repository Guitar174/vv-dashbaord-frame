1.1 Case Study: Evaluating Earthquake Damage

The idea In 2015, the Gorkha earthquake (magnitude 7.8) hit Nepal. Two types of reinforced concrete buildings are compared:
  • Engineered — built following seismic design codes
  • Non-engineered — built without following those codes
No problem to solve yet — this page just sets up the case study.
Example — what "seismic design codes" actually require Concretely, an engineered building typically has: extra steel reinforcement (rebar) tying beams and columns together, flexible joints that let the structure sway a little instead of snapping, and a foundation designed to distribute shaking forces evenly. A non-engineered building might use the same concrete and bricks, but skip the rebar spacing rules and flexible joints — so it's stiffer and more likely to crack suddenly rather than flex. That structural difference is exactly what the study is trying to measure by comparing damage outcomes.

Earthquake Damage — the two-way table

The problem "Success" = minor damage, "Failure" = severe damage. Out of 1,812 total buildings — Engineered: 716 success / 195 failure. Non-engineered: 668 success / 233 failure. Which type is better overall?
Step-by-step
Step 1 — find each row's total.
Engineered: 716 + 195 = 911
Non-engineered: 668 + 233 = 901
Step 2 — divide successes by that row's total.
Engineered success rate = 716 ÷ 911 = 78.6%
Non-engineered success rate = 668 ÷ 901 = 74.1%
Step 3 — compare.
78.6% > 74.1%, so at this stage Engineered looks better by about 4.5 percentage points.

The explanation

The problem Non-engineered wins in high-rise AND in low-rise, but Engineered won overall. How is that possible?
Step-by-step reasoning
Step 1. Look at how the two building types are split by height. Engineered: 660 high-rise / 251 low-rise → about 72% of Engineered buildings are high-rise. Non-engineered: 193 high-rise / 708 low-rise → about 79% of Non-engineered buildings are low-rise.
Step 2. Low-rise buildings survive earthquakes better than high-rise buildings, regardless of construction type. So Non-engineered's overall rate gets pulled up (mostly low-rise = "easier" category), while Engineered's overall rate gets pulled down (mostly high-rise = "harder" category).
Step 3. Lumping everyone together in the Page 2 table doesn't just compare construction type — it secretly also compares "mostly-high-rise" vs "mostly-low-rise" groups. That hidden mix flipped the answer. Building height is a confounding variable.

Simpson's Paradox

The idea Simpson's Paradox: an observed relationship between two variables can change — or even reverse — once a third variable is taken into account. In observational studies, a confounding variable can obscure the true relationship.
The problem Looking only at the "all buildings" table (716/195 vs 668/233), does that data show a "real" difference between the two construction types?
Step-by-step answer
Step 1. The all-buildings table mixes two very different sub-populations (mostly-high-rise Engineered vs mostly-low-rise Non-engineered).
Step 2. Since the subgroup comparisons (high-rise vs high-rise, low-rise vs low-rise) both favor Non-engineered, the "Engineered wins" signal in the combined table is an artifact of unequal group composition, not a real construction-quality difference.
Answer: No — the combined table does not show a trustworthy difference; it's confounded by height.

1.3 Observational Studies and Sampling Strategies

The idea Running example: research question is whether people become better runners just by running. The sample is a group of adult women who recently joined a running group. If that sample was randomly chosen, results can generalize to the broader population of adult women.
A second example, to make it click A phone company wants to know the average battery life of its new phone model. The population is every one of the 2 million units sold. Testing all 2 million is impossible, so the company tests a sample of 500 randomly chosen units from the warehouse. If those 500 are representative, their average battery life (say, 11.2 hours) becomes the company's estimate for all 2 million phones — the same logic as the running example, just with phones instead of people.
Page 15

Census

The idea A census means surveying the entire population instead of a sample. Three problems with taking a census: (1) hard-to-find individuals are often systematically different from the rest of the population, (2) populations change constantly so a "perfect" measurement is never possible, (3) a census can be more complex/costly than sampling.
Real-world example The U.S. Census Bureau tries to count every single resident every 10 years — a true census. Even with a massive government budget, it consistently undercounts homeless people, undocumented immigrants, and people who move frequently, precisely because those groups are hardest to locate (problem #1 above). And by the time results are published, some people have already died, been born, or moved (problem #2) — so even a "complete" census is already slightly out of date the moment it finishes.

Exploratory Analysis to Inference / Sampling Bias

The soup analogy Tasting a spoonful = exploratory analysis. Concluding the whole pot needs salt = inference. For the inference to be valid, stir the pot first (make the sample representative).
Three red flags to check for bias
  • Convenience sample — only easy-to-reach people included
  • Non-response — only a small fraction of the randomly sampled people actually respond
  • Voluntary response — only people with strong opinions bother to respond

Observational Studies / Prospective vs. Retrospective

The idea In an observational study, researchers don't interfere with how the data arise — results can show association, not causation. Prospective studies track individuals forward as events unfold (e.g. the Nurses' Health Study, since 1976). Retrospective studies look backward at data already collected (e.g. reviewing old medical records).

The idea Statistical methods rely on randomness — non-random data collection makes the resulting estimates unreliable. Simple random sample: every case is selected from the population with no implied connection between selected points (like drawing names from a hat).

The idea Stratified sample: split the population into similar groups (strata), then take a simple random sample from each. Cluster sample: population is split into (usually non-homogeneous) clusters; randomly select a few whole clusters and sample every individual inside them — cheaper to run.

Principles of Experimental Design

The idea — remember "C-R-R-B" Control (compare to a control group), Randomize (randomly assign subjects), Replicate (large enough sample, or repeat the study), Block (group subjects by a known factor first, then randomize within each group).
The problem Design an experiment testing whether energy gel makes runners faster, given that pros and amateurs might react differently.
Step-by-step
Step 1 — block. Split runners into "pro" and "amateur" groups first, since pro status is suspected to affect the outcome.
Step 2 — randomize within each block. Within the pro group, randomly assign gel vs. no gel. Do the same within the amateur group.
Result: Pro/amateur status is now balanced evenly across the gel and no-gel groups, so it can't secretly skew the results.

Blocking vs. Explanatory Variables (practice)

The problem A study tests light and noise levels on exam scores, and also wants both genders equally represented in each group. Which option correctly labels the variables?
Step-by-step
Step 1. Ask "can the researcher directly control it?" Light level and noise level — yes, the researcher sets them → explanatory variables.
Step 2. Gender — the researcher can't control it, but wants it balanced across groups → blocking variable.
Step 3. Exam performance — this is what's being measured → response variable.
Answer: (B) 2 explanatory variables (light, noise), 1 blocking variable (gender), 1 response variable (exam performance)

More Experimental Design Terminology

Vocabulary Placebo: fake treatment, often the control in medical studies. Placebo effect: subjects improve just from believing they got real treatment. Blinding: subjects don't know their own group. Double-blind: neither subjects nor the researchers interacting with them know group assignment.
The problem What's the main difference between observational studies and experiments?
Step-by-step
Step 1. Ask: "Did the researcher randomly assign subjects to groups?" Experiments do this on purpose; observational studies just watch what happens without assigning anything.
Answer: (C) Most experiments use random assignment, while observational studies do not.

Random Assignment vs. Random Sampling

The big final idea Random assignment → lets you say "this caused that." Random sampling → lets you say "this applies to everyone." Worked example: a researcher randomly selects 200 students from the whole school (random sampling ✓) and randomly assigns them to "study with music" vs. "study in silence" (random assignment ✓). Because both boxes are checked, if the music group scores higher, the researcher can say music caused the improvement, and that conclusion applies to the whole school.

Mean

Purpose & when to use it The mean is a single number that summarizes the "center" of a numerical dataset by balancing all the values against each other. Use it when the data is roughly symmetric and free of extreme outliers — an income dataset with one billionaire in it is exactly when you should reach for the median instead (see Pages 10–12).
The idea The sample mean (x̄) is calculated from the observed values x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ. The population mean (µ) is computed the same way but is usually unavailable since population data are rarely collected in full. The sample mean is a point estimate of µ — imperfect, but usually good if the sample is representative.
How to compute a sample mean — step by step
Step 1. Add up all n observed values: x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ.
Step 2. Divide that sum by n (the sample size).
Example: data = 4, 6, 8. Sum = 18, n = 3, mean = 18 ÷ 3 = 6.
Stacked Dot Plot — purpose & when to use it Purpose: plot every individual data point as its own dot, stacked at its value, so you can see the exact raw data and its clustering. Taller stacks show where observations are more common. Use it when the dataset is small-to-moderate (roughly under 50 points) — beyond that, individual dots overlap too much and a histogram (Page 2) becomes the better tool.
Page 2

Histograms — bin width

Purpose & when to use it A histogram groups numerical data into bins and shows the count (or density) in each bin as a bar, revealing the overall shape of a distribution — its center, spread, modality, and skew. Use it when you have a numerical dataset large enough (dozens to thousands of points) that a stacked dot plot would be too cluttered to read. It's usually the very first thing you plot before choosing which summary statistics (mean vs. median, SD vs. IQR) are trustworthy for that data.
The problem Several histograms of the same extracurricular-hours data, drawn with different bin widths. Which are useful? Which reveal too much (noisy)? Which hide too much (over-smoothed)?
How to judge bin width
Too narrow (many tiny bins) → the histogram looks jagged/noisy, showing random fluctuation instead of the real shape.
Too wide (few huge bins) → real structure (like a second peak) gets smoothed away and hidden.
Just right → a bin width that's narrow enough to show the true shape but wide enough to smooth out noise. There's no single formula — you try a few widths and pick the one that tells the clearest story.
Worked example 20 students report extracurricular hours per week: 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 10, 12, 14, 15, 20, 25.
  • Bin width = 1 (20 tiny bins) → almost every bin holds just 1–3 students; the histogram looks jagged and spiky, with gaps — too noisy to see the real pattern.
  • Bin width = 5 (bins: 0–5, 5–10, 10–15, 15–20, 20–25) → counts land at roughly 4, 9, 4, 1, 1 students per bin — a clean, clearly right-skewed shape emerges: most students cluster around 5–10 hours, with a thin tail stretching out to 25.
  • Bin width = 25 (one giant bin) → all 20 students collapse into a single bar — you can no longer see any shape at all.
Bin width = 5 tells the clearest story here — narrow enough to reveal the skew, wide enough to smooth out the bin-to-bin noise.

Shape of a Distribution: Modality & Skewness

Purpose & when to use it Describing modality and skewness in words is how you translate a picture (histogram/dot plot) into a decision: it tells you which summary statistics to trust later. Use it every time, right after plotting a histogram and before computing mean/SD or median/IQR — skewed or multimodal data changes which numbers are meaningful (see Pages 10–12).
The problem For a given histogram, is it unimodal, bimodal/multimodal, or uniform? And is it right-skewed, left-skewed, or symmetric?
Step-by-step — how to read modality and skew
Modality: imagine draping limp spaghetti over the bars — the smooth curve it forms shows the peaks. One hump → unimodal. Two humps → bimodal. Flat, no real hump → uniform.
Skewness: find the side with the long thin tail — that's the direction of skew. Long tail stretching right → right-skewed. Long tail stretching left → left-skewed. Roughly mirror-image on both sides → symmetric.
Worked examples of each shape
  • Bimodal — exam scores in a class where half the students studied hard and half didn't: one cluster of scores around 85–95, another cluster around 50–60, with few scores in between. Two separate humps.
  • Right-skewed — reaction times in a game: most players react in 0.3–0.6 seconds, but a few distracted players take 2–3 seconds. The bulk of data sits on the left with a long thin tail stretching right.
  • Left-skewed — score out of 100 on an easy quiz: most students score 85–100, but a few who missed class score 20–40, dragging a thin tail out to the left.
  • Uniform — the last digit of people's phone numbers: 0 through 9 are all roughly equally likely, so the histogram is close to flat.

Unusual Observations

Purpose & when to use it Scanning for unusual observations catches data-entry errors and genuinely rare cases before they distort your analysis. Use it as a standard first check on any new dataset, before computing mean, SD, or building a model — a single typo (like "500" instead of "50") can silently wreck a mean and SD.
The problem Are there any unusual observations or potential outliers in the extracurricular-hours histogram? How would you describe its overall shape?
Step-by-step
Step 1. Scan the histogram for isolated bars far from the main cluster of data — those are candidate outliers.
Step 2. Describe the shape using the vocabulary from Page 3: modality (uni/bi/multi/uniform) + skew direction (right/left/symmetric). For a typical "hours spent on extracurriculars" dataset, expect a right-skewed, unimodal shape — most students cluster at low-to-moderate hours, with a thin tail of students spending unusually many hours.
Example. Using the Page 2 dataset (2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 10, 12, 14, 15, 20, 25), most values sit between 2 and 15. The value 25 sits well past the rest of the pack, on its own past the next-largest value (20) — that's the kind of isolated, far-flung point you'd flag as a potential outlier.

Variance & Standard Deviation

Purpose & when to use it These measure how spread out the data is around the mean. Variance is in squared units (hard to interpret directly); standard deviation converts it back to the data's original units, which is why SD is the one usually reported and discussed. Use them when the data is roughly symmetric with no extreme outliers — like the mean they're built from, both variance and SD are sensitive to outliers, so for skewed/outlier-heavy data use IQR (Page 7) instead.
The idea Variance is roughly the average squared deviation from the mean. Standard deviation is the square root of the variance, in the same units as the original data. The slide's worked numbers come from a sleep-hours dataset (n = 217); the handwritten scratch work on the page is that specific calculation, but the method below works for any dataset.
Step-by-step — how to compute variance and SD by hand
Step 1. Compute the sample mean x̄ (see Page 1).
Step 2. For each data point xᵢ, compute the deviation (xᵢ − x̄), then square it.
Step 3. Add up all the squared deviations.
Step 4. Divide by (n − 1), not n — this is the sample variance, s².
Step 5. Take the square root of s² to get the standard deviation, s.
Example: data = 4, 6, 8. Mean = 6. Deviations: (4−6)²=4, (6−6)²=0, (8−6)²=4. Sum = 8. Variance = 8 ÷ (3−1) = 4. SD = √4 = 2.
Why square the deviations? If you just added the raw deviations (xᵢ − x̄), positive and negative differences would cancel out to zero every time. Squaring makes everything positive so the spread doesn't disappear.
Why divide by (n − 1)? Using the sample mean (instead of the true population mean) to compute deviations makes the raw average slightly underestimate the true spread. Dividing by (n − 1) instead of n corrects for that bias — it's called Bessel's correction.

Median, Quartiles, and IQR

Purpose & when to use it The median and IQR summarize center and spread using the data's rank order instead of arithmetic averages, which makes them resistant to extreme values. Use them when the distribution is skewed or contains outliers — household income, home prices, and reaction times with slow outliers are classic cases where median/IQR give a more honest picture than mean/SD.
The idea The median splits ordered data in half — it's the 50th percentile. Q1 = 25th percentile, Q2 = median, Q3 = 75th percentile. IQR = Q3 − Q1 is the span of the middle 50% of the data.
Step-by-step — finding the median, Q1, Q3, and IQR
Step 1. Sort the data from smallest to largest.
Step 2. Median = the middle value (if n is odd) or the average of the two middle values (if n is even).
Step 3. Q1 = median of the lower half of the data (not including the overall median if n is odd). Q3 = median of the upper half.
Step 4. IQR = Q3 − Q1.
Example: sorted data = 2, 4, 5, 7, 9 (n=5, odd). Median = middle value = 5. Lower half = {2,4} → Q1 = (2+4)/2 = 3. Upper half = {7,9} → Q3 = (7+9)/2 = 8. IQR = 8 − 3 = 5.

Box Plot Anatomy

Purpose & when to use it A box plot packs the five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) into one compact picture and flags potential outliers automatically. Use it when you want to compare the spread and center of several groups side by side (e.g. test scores across 5 different classes) — box plots line up neatly next to each other in a way histograms don't, though they hide finer details like modality that a histogram would show.
The idea The box in a box plot represents the middle 50% of the data (from Q1 to Q3), and the thick line inside the box marks the median. This is the reference diagram for Page 9's whisker/outlier calculation.
The problem Build a box plot from this dataset: 3, 7, 8, 12, 13, 18, 22 (already sorted, n = 7).
Step-by-step
Step 1 — median. n = 7 (odd), so the median is the 4th value: 12.
Step 2 — Q1. Lower half (excluding the median) = {3, 7, 8} → its median is the middle value: Q1 = 7.
Step 3 — Q3. Upper half = {13, 18, 22} → its median is the middle value: Q3 = 18.
Step 4 — draw it. Box spans Q1=7 to Q3=18, with a line inside at the median (12). Left whisker reaches out to the minimum (3); right whisker reaches out to the maximum (22) — both are within the 1.5×IQR reach worked out the same way as Page 9, so neither end is an outlier here.
Page 9

Whiskers and Outliers

Purpose & when to use it The 1.5×IQR rule gives a consistent, objective cutoff for flagging potential outliers, instead of eyeballing which points "look" extreme. Use it any time you have a box plot and need to decide whether an extreme point should be investigated, excluded, or just noted — it's the standard rule most statistical software uses to draw box plot whiskers automatically.
The problem Given IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 20 − 10 = 10, find the maximum reach of the upper and lower whiskers, and explain what counts as an outlier.
Step-by-step
Step 1. Whiskers can extend up to 1.5×IQR past each quartile. 1.5 × 10 = 15.
Step 2 — upper reach. Q3 + 1.5×IQR = 20 + 15 = 35.
Step 3 — lower reach. Q1 − 1.5×IQR = 10 − 15 = −5.
Step 4. Any observation beyond 35 or below −5 is a potential outlier. Outliers matter because a single extreme point can heavily distort the mean, SD, and any analysis built on them, leading to wrong conclusions.

Extreme Observations / Robust Statistics

Purpose & when to use it This thought experiment is the "why" behind the mean/median and SD/IQR choice: it demonstrates directly which statistics move when you change one extreme value. Use this test mentally whenever you're unsure if a statistic is trustworthy — ask "if I swapped out the biggest or smallest value, would this number barely move (robust) or swing wildly (not robust)?"
The problem How would the mean, median, SD, and IQR of household income change if the largest value in the dataset were replaced with $10 million? What if the smallest value were replaced with $10 million?
Step-by-step reasoning
Replacing the largest value with $10 million: the mean jumps up a lot (it's an average, so one huge number drags it up); SD explodes too (that point is now far from the mean). The median barely moves — it only cares about the middle position, not the extreme value's size. IQR is unaffected unless it changes which values fall in the middle 50%.
Replacing the smallest value with $10 million: the mean and SD swing wildly again (now there's an unusually high value where a low one used to be); median and IQR are again largely unaffected.
Conclusion: mean and SD are sensitive to extreme values; median and IQR are "robust" — they resist being pulled around by outliers.

Robust Statistics / Mean vs. Median

Purpose & when to use it This is the decision rule that ties Pages 6–11 together: symmetric, outlier-free data → report mean and SD; skewed or outlier-heavy data → report median and IQR. Use it as the last step whenever you're about to summarize a numerical variable — check the histogram's shape first (Page 3), then pick the matching pair of statistics.
The problem To estimate a "typical" student's household income, would you trust the mean or the median more?
Step-by-step
Step 1. Household income is typically right-skewed — most households earn a moderate amount, but a few earn extremely high incomes, stretching a long tail to the right.
Step 2. As shown on Page 10, the mean gets pulled toward that long tail, so it overstates what's "typical." The median stays put in the middle of the pack.
Answer: Median — it's more robust and more representative of a "typical" household for skewed data like income.

Mean vs. Median and Skew Direction

Purpose & when to use it This rule works as a shortcut in both directions: if you know the skew, you can predict where the mean sits relative to the median (this page); or, if you're only given the mean and median (no picture), you can infer the skew direction without ever seeing a histogram. Use it when a dataset is described only with summary numbers and you need to reason about its shape.
The problem For % of class time spent taking notes vs. on social media, the median is at 80%. Where's the mean — above, about equal to, or below the median?
Step-by-step
Step 1. Rule: right-skewed → mean > median; left-skewed → mean < median; symmetric → mean ≈ median.
Step 2. Since the median is 80% (most students spend most of their time taking notes), there's likely a small tail of students spending very little time on notes (i.e., a lot on social media) — that's a long tail toward 0%, on the left.
Answer: (c) mean < median — the low-percentage tail pulls the mean down below 80%.

Transformations for Skewed Data

Purpose & when to use it A log transform compresses large values much more than small ones, pulling in a long right tail so the data looks closer to symmetric. Use it when data is extremely right-skewed with all-positive values (income, population, games attended, followers) and you need it to behave better for plotting or for statistical methods that assume roughly symmetric data. Don't use it when the result needs to stay easily interpretable to a non-technical audience, since "log dollars" means little to most readers.
The idea When data are extremely skewed (e.g. number of basketball games attended), a log transformation can make modeling easier — outliers become far less prominent afterward. Downside: the log of a measured variable is often hard to interpret meaningfully.
The problem What other variables would you expect to be extremely skewed (and might benefit from a log transform)?
Examples Household income, company revenue, city population, number of social media followers, home prices — all have a long right tail of unusually large values, the same shape as the basketball-games example.
Worked example — before and after the log transform Games attended by 6 students: 2, 3, 3, 5, 8, 60. The raw values span from 2 to 60 — a range of 58, almost entirely driven by that one student who attended 60 games.
Taking the natural log of each value: ln(2)=0.69, ln(3)=1.10, ln(3)=1.10, ln(5)=1.61, ln(8)=2.08, ln(60)=4.09.
Now the values span from 0.69 to 4.09 — a range of only 3.4. The extreme student no longer dominates the spread; the data is far easier to model and plot. That's the trade-off: better-behaved numbers, but "4.09" isn't something you can explain to someone as intuitively as "60 games."

Bar Plots / Choosing the Right Proportion

Purpose & when to use it A bar plot displays counts or proportions for a single categorical variable, with one bar per category and visible gaps between bars (unlike a histogram). Use plain counts when comparing categories within one group of a fixed size; switch to a relative frequency bar plot (proportions) the moment you're comparing groups of different sizes — like comparing 90 females to 60 males below, where raw counts alone would be misleading.
The problem Using the Page 14 contingency table, find % of females looking for a spouse and % of males looking for a spouse — is there a relationship between gender and looking for a spouse?
Step-by-step method, worked with Page 14's numbers
Step 1 — % females looking. 60 females said yes, out of 90 females total: 60 ÷ 90 = 66.7%.
Step 2 — % males looking. 15 males said yes, out of 60 males total: 15 ÷ 60 = 25.0%.
Step 3 — compare. 66.7% vs. 25.0% is a big gap — about two-thirds of females are looking for a spouse, versus only a quarter of males. That gap suggests gender and spouse-searching are associated in this (made-up) dataset — if the two percentages had come out close (say 60% vs. 58%), that would instead point toward independence.
Bar plot vs. histogram A bar plot compares separate categories with gaps between bars (categorical data); a histogram shows a continuous numerical distribution with touching bars, no gaps. A bar plot of proportions instead of counts is a relative frequency bar plot.

Segmented Bar and Mosaic Plots

Purpose & when to use it These three all visualize two categorical variables together, but answer slightly different questions. Stacked — use when you care about total group sizes (e.g. "how many females total?"). Side-by-side — use when you want to compare exact segment counts precisely (harder to do when segments are stacked on top of each other). Standardized stacked — use when comparing the internal composition/proportions of groups that differ a lot in size, so a small group doesn't look "insignificant" just because its bar is short.
The problem What are the differences between a stacked bar plot, a side-by-side bar plot, and a standardized stacked bar plot?
Step-by-step comparison
Stacked bar plot — one bar per group, segments stacked on top of each other showing raw counts.
Side-by-side bar plot — the same count information, but each segment gets its own bar placed next to the others instead of stacked — easier to compare segment sizes directly.
Standardized stacked bar plot — like the stacked version, but every bar is rescaled to 100% height, showing proportions instead of counts — best for comparing composition across groups of very different sizes.
Worked example, using Page 14's table
  • Stacked: a "Female" bar reaching up to 90, split into a 60-tall "Yes" segment and a 30-tall "No" segment on top; a "Male" bar reaching up to 60, split into 15 "Yes" + 45 "No."
  • Side-by-side: four separate bars — Female-Yes (60), Female-No (30), Male-Yes (15), Male-No (45) — placed next to each other so you can compare heights directly.
  • Standardized stacked: both the Female and Male bars are stretched to the same 100% height. Female becomes 66.7% "Yes" / 33.3% "No"; Male becomes 25% "Yes" / 75% "No." Even though there are more females (90) than males (60) in the raw counts, this version makes the proportion difference (66.7% vs. 25%) the easiest of the three to see at a glance.

Mosaic Plots vs. Pie Charts

Purpose & when to use it Pie chart — shows how a single categorical variable's total splits into shares of one whole. Best with a small number of categories (roughly ≤5); beyond that, thin wedges become hard to compare by eye. Mosaic plot — extends the same idea to two categorical variables at once, encoding one variable in rectangle width and the other in rectangle height, so it can also reveal association between them (something a pie chart can't do at all). Use a mosaic plot whenever you'd otherwise reach for a standardized stacked bar plot (Page 16) but want group sizes visible too.
The problem Compare a mosaic plot to a pie chart for the same categorical data (mammal species by order) — which order has the lowest percentage of species?
How to read each chart
Mosaic plot: each category is a rectangle whose width (and sometimes height) is proportional to its share of the data — the narrowest rectangle is the smallest category.
Pie chart: each category is a wedge whose angle is proportional to its share — the smallest, thinnest wedge is the smallest category.
To answer "which order has the lowest percentage," find the narrowest mosaic rectangle or the thinnest pie wedge in the actual chart image above.
Worked example Say 4 categories make up a dataset: A = 40%, B = 30%, C = 20%, D = 10%. In a mosaic plot, the four rectangles sit side by side with widths in a 4:3:2:1 ratio — D's rectangle is visibly the thinnest sliver. In a pie chart, the same data becomes wedges of 144°, 108°, 72°, and 36° (each % × 3.6°) — D's wedge is the smallest, at just 36° out of 360°. Both charts correctly show D as smallest; mosaic plots tend to make small differences (like C at 20% vs. B at 30%) easier to compare precisely, since comparing rectangle widths is easier for the eye than comparing wedge angles.
