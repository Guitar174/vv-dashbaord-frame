Page 1

1.1 Case Study: Evaluating Earthquake Damage

Sets up the case study: the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal (magnitude 7.8). Two types of reinforced concrete buildings are being compared:
  • Engineered — built following seismic design codes
  • Non-engineered — built without following those codes
Page 2

Earthquake Damage — the two-way table

"Success" = minor damage; "Failure" = severe damage. Out of 1,812 total buildings, the table gives success/failure counts for Engineered (716 success, 195 failure) vs. Non-engineered (668 success, 233 failure). The question: which type is better overall?
Page 3

Introducing a third variable — building height

We also know each building's height: high-rise or low-rise (853 of the 1,812 are high-rise). Question: does the "better" building type change once you look only at high-rises? Table: Engineered 545/115, Non-engineered 165/28.
Page 4

Low-rise buildings only

959 of the 1,812 are low-rise. Table: Engineered 171/80, Non-engineered 503/205. The page then asks students to fill in blanks: which type wins overall, which wins for high-rise, which wins for low-rise — setting up the paradox.
Page 5

What's the explanation? (blank worksheet tables)

Repeats the three tables (all buildings, high-rise, low-rise) as blank worksheets — students are meant to compute the totals and success rates themselves in class, then compare.
Page 6

The explanation

Engineered looks better overall mainly because it adheres to seismic codes — but non-engineered buildings were more common among low-rises, which naturally survive better regardless of construction type. Building height is a confounding variable: it affects both which construction type a building tends to be, and how much damage it takes. Once height is accounted for, non-engineered actually wins in both subgroups.
Page 7

Simpson's Paradox

Names the phenomenon: Simpson's Paradox — an observed relationship between two variables can change or reverse when a third variable is considered. In observational studies, a confounding variable can hide the "true" relationship. The page asks: looking at the combined (all-buildings) table, does it show a "real" difference between the two construction types?
Page 8

Generalizing the results / start of 1.2 Data Basics

Closing question for the case study: can these earthquake results be generalized to buildings of all heights? This transitions into the next topic, 1.2 Data Basics.

Practice: area codes / Relationships among variables

Practice question: what type of variable is a telephone area code? (Answer: categorical, nominal — averaging area codes is meaningless.) Then introduces looking for relationships between two numerical variables, e.g. hours of study per week vs. GPA.
Page 12

Spotting unusual points / possum scatterplot

Asks students to spot unusual (outlier) points in the study-hours-vs-GPA scatterplot. Then introduces a new scatterplot: head length vs. skull width in possums, and asks what can correctly be said about the relationship.
Page 13

Associated vs. Independent variables

Multiple-choice on the possum scatterplot — correct answer: head length and skull width are positively associated (you cannot claim causation from a graph alone). Defines: associated (dependent) variables show a connection; independent variables show no connection.
Page 14

1.3 Observational Studies and Sampling Strategies

Defines population vs. sample using a running example: research question is whether people become better runners just by running. The sample is a group of adult women who recently joined a running group; if randomly sampled, the results can generalize to the broader population of adult women.
Page 15

Census

A census means surveying the entire population instead of a sample. Problems with censuses: hard-to-find individuals are often systematically different from the rest; populations change constantly so a "perfect" measurement is never possible; and a census can be more complex/costly than sampling.
Page 16

Exploratory Analysis to Inference / Sampling Bias

The soup analogy: tasting a spoonful = exploratory analysis; concluding the whole pot needs salt = inference. The sample must represent the population (stir the pot first). Three bias types defined:
  • Convenience sample — only easy-to-reach people included
  • Non-response — few of the randomly sampled people actually respond
  • Voluntary response — only people with strong opinions respond
Page 17

Sampling Bias Example: Landon vs. FDR (1936)

The Literary Digest polled ~10 million people, got 2.4 million responses, and predicted Landon would win big over FDR. In reality, FDR won with 62% of the vote — the magazine was discredited and later shut down.
Page 18

What went wrong — large samples aren't enough

The poll sampled the magazine's own readers, registered car owners, and phone owners — all wealthier than average during the Great Depression, so the sample skewed Republican and wasn't representative. Lesson: a huge sample size (2.4 million) cannot fix a biased sample. Unstirred soup tastes wrong no matter how big the spoon.
Page 19

Observational Studies / Prospective vs. Retrospective

In an observational study, researchers don't interfere with how the data arise — results can show association, not causation. Prospective studies track individuals forward in time as events unfold (e.g. the Nurses' Health Study, since 1976). Retrospective studies look backward at data already collected (e.g. reviewing old medical records).
Page 20

Obtaining Good Samples / Simple Random Sample

Statistical methods rely on randomness — non-random data collection makes the resulting estimates unreliable. Simple random sample: every case is selected from the population with no implied connection between selected points (like drawing names from a hat).
Page 21

Stratified Sample / Cluster Sample

Stratified sample: split the population into similar groups (strata), then take a simple random sample from each. Cluster sample: population is split into (usually non-homogeneous) clusters; randomly select a few whole clusters and sample every individual inside them — cheaper to run.
Page 22

Multistage Sample / start of 1.4 Experiments

Multistage sample: randomly select clusters, then take a simple random sample of individuals from within those selected clusters (not everyone in the cluster). This closes out the sampling section and moves into 1.4 Experiments.
Page 23

Principles of Experimental Design

Four principles (remember "C-R-R-B"): Control (compare to a control group), Randomize (randomly assign subjects/sample from population), Replicate (large enough sample, or repeat the study), Block (group subjects by a known factor first, then randomize within each group). Worked example: testing energy gel on runners, blocking by pro/amateur status so that factor is balanced across treatment and control.
Page 24

Blocking vs. Explanatory Variables (practice)

Explanatory variables (factors) are conditions the researcher can control/impose. Blocking variables are characteristics subjects already have that the researcher can't control but wants balanced. Practice question (light/noise/gender on exam scores) — correct answer: 2 explanatory variables (light, noise), 1 blocking variable (gender), 1 response variable (exam performance).
Page 25

More Experimental Design Terminology

Placebo: fake treatment, often the control in medical studies. Placebo effect: subjects improve just from believing they got real treatment. Blinding: subjects don't know their own group. Double-blind: neither subjects nor the researchers interacting with them know group assignment. Practice question — correct answer: most experiments use random assignment, while observational studies do not.
Page 26

Random Assignment vs. Random Sampling

Closing title slide for the chapter's big final idea: distinguishing what random assignment (lets you claim causation) buys you versus what random sampling (lets you generalize to everyone) buys you — the 2×2 framework worked out in the companion study guide.
Page 1

Mean

The sample mean (x̄) is calculated from the observed values x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ. The population mean (µ) is computed the same way but is usually unavailable since population data are rarely collected in full. The sample mean is a point estimate of µ — imperfect, but usually good if the sample is representative. Introduces the stacked dot plot: taller stacks show where observations are more common, helping you judge the center and shape of a distribution.
Page 2

Histograms — bin width

Histograms show data density — taller bars = more common values — and are convenient for describing the shape of a distribution. The chosen bin width can change the story the histogram tells: too wide hides detail, too narrow reveals noise. Example uses extracurricular hours data.
Page 3

Shape of a Distribution: Modality & Skewness

Modality: does the histogram have one prominent peak (unimodal), several (bimodal/multimodal), or none (uniform)? Trick for judging it: imagine draping limp spaghetti over the bars to see the smooth curve. Skewness: is it right-skewed, left-skewed, or symmetric? A histogram is skewed toward the side with the long tail.
Page 4

Unusual Observations

Asks whether there are any unusual observations or potential outliers, and to describe the shape of the distribution of hours per week students spend on extracurricular activities.
Page 5

Commonly Observed Shapes (practice)

Summary chart of shapes: Modality — unimodal, bimodal, multimodal, uniform. Skewness — right skew, left skew, symmetric. Practice question: which variable is uniformly distributed? Answer: (d) birthdays of classmates (day of the month) — roughly equally likely across all days, unlike weights, salaries, or house prices which cluster and skew.
Page 6

Variance & Standard Deviation

Variance is roughly the average squared deviation from the mean (worked example: amount of sleep students get, n = 217). Squaring is used so positive and negative deviations don't cancel out; dividing by (n − 1) instead of n corrects for bias when estimating from a sample. Standard deviation is the square root of the variance and shares the same units as the data — most data fall within about 3 standard deviations of the mean.
Page 7

Median, Quartiles, and IQR

The median splits ordered data in half (average of the two middle values if n is even) — it's the 50th percentile. Q1 = 25th percentile, Q2 = median = 50th percentile, Q3 = 75th percentile. The middle 50% of data lies between Q1 and Q3, a span called the interquartile range: IQR = Q3 − Q1.
Page 8

Box Plot Anatomy

The box in a box plot represents the middle 50% of the data (Q1 to Q3), and the thick line inside the box marks the median.
Page 9

Whiskers and Outliers

Whiskers extend up to 1.5×IQR from the quartiles: max upper reach = Q3 + 1.5×IQR, max lower reach = Q1 − 1.5×IQR. Worked example: IQR = 20 − 10 = 10, so upper reach = 20 + 15 = 35, lower reach = 10 − 15 = −5. A potential outlier is any observation beyond those reaches. Outliers matter because a single extreme point can heavily distort an analysis and lead to wrong conclusions.
Page 10

Extreme Observations / Robust Statistics

Thought experiment: how would mean, median, SD, and IQR of household income change if the largest value were replaced with $10 million? What if the smallest value were? (Mean and SD swing heavily; median and IQR barely move — this motivates the idea of "robust" statistics.)
Page 11

Robust Statistics / Mean vs. Median

Median and IQR are more robust to skewness and outliers than mean and SD. For skewed distributions, median/IQR better describe center and spread; for symmetric distributions, mean/SD are usually more informative. In a symmetric distribution, mean ≈ median. Question: for typical household income (usually skewed), would you trust the mean or the median more? (Median.)
Page 12

Mean vs. Median and Skew Direction

Rule: right-skewed → mean > median; left-skewed → mean < median. Practice question: for % of class time spent taking notes vs. on social media, with median at 80%, where's the mean? This is left-skewed (a long tail of low values pulls the mean down) → (c) mean < median.
Page 13

Transformations for Skewed Data

When data are extremely skewed (e.g. number of basketball games attended), a log transformation can make modeling easier — outliers become far less prominent after transforming. Downside: the log of a measured variable is often hard to interpret meaningfully.
Page 14

2.2 Considering Categorical Data — Contingency Tables

A contingency table summarizes data for two categorical variables. Example: students' gender crossed with whether they're looking for a spouse while in college.
Page 15

Bar Plots / Choosing the Right Proportion

A bar plot displays a single categorical variable; if it shows proportions instead of counts it's a relative frequency bar plot. Bar plots compare separate categories with gaps between bars; histograms show a continuous numerical distribution with touching bars, no gaps. To compare gender vs. looking-for-a-spouse, use row proportions (% of females looking, % of males looking).
Page 16

Segmented Bar and Mosaic Plots

Three ways to plot two categorical variables together: stacked bar plot (counts, stacked), side-by-side bar plot (counts, bars next to each other), and standardized stacked bar plot (proportions instead of raw counts).
Page 17

Mosaic Plots vs. Pie Charts

Compares mosaic plots to pie charts for categorical data, including an example about mammal species by order — asking which order has the lowest percentage of species.

2.3 Case Study: Structural Failures

200 residential buildings were examined to see whether foundation soil type (clay vs. sand) is associated with major structural failures; 70 of the 200 had failures. Question posed: is this study an observational study or an experiment? (Observational — soil type wasn't randomly assigned to buildings.)

Data: Clay vs. Sand Foundations

Table: Clay foundation — 50 cracked, 30 not cracked (80 total, 62.5% cracked). Sand foundation — 20 cracked, 100 not cracked (120 total, ≈16.7% cracked). At first glance, clay foundations look far more prone to cracking. This sets up the next case study on earthquake-caused building damage.
