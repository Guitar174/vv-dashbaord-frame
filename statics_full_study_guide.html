What is Mechanics? (Introduction)

PurposeMechanics exists because engineers need a formal way to predict what happens to an object ("body") when forces act on it — without this, you couldn't design anything that has to hold weight or resist a push.
When to useAnytime you're deciding whether a physics problem belongs to "mechanics" at all — e.g., predicting whether a bookshelf bracket will bend under a stack of books is a mechanics question; predicting the bookshelf's paint color is not.
ExampleA 10 kg box sits on a table. The "body" is the box; the "force" is its own weight (mg = 10 × 9.81 = 98.1 N) pushing down, and the table pushes back up with an equal 98.1 N. Mechanics is the toolset that lets you write down and solve that force balance instead of just guessing.
Watch outStudents confuse "mechanics" (the science) with "a mechanic" (a car repair person) — unrelated meanings of the same word.

What is Mechanics? (Introduction) — categories

PurposeTo split "mechanics" into manageable sub-fields, because a body that doesn't deform (rigid) needs different math than one that stretches or a fluid that flows.
When to useUse this map to know which course to reach for: analyzing a bridge that stays rigid → Statics; a car crash where things accelerate → Dynamics; a beam that bends and might crack → Mechanics of Materials (deformable bodies); water flow in a pipe → Fluid Mechanics.
ExampleA parked car (v = 0, constant) is a Statics problem. The same car accelerating from a red light is a Dynamics problem, because now a ≠ 0.
Watch outStudents think "Statics" means "nothing moves ever." It actually means net force and acceleration are zero — an object cruising at constant velocity (v = constant ≠ 0) is also in static equilibrium.

Statics, Dynamics and Mechanics of Materials

PurposeStatics exists to answer "why do things break?" — by finding the forces acting on a structure from the outside, you can then (in a later course) find the internal stresses that cause failure.
When to useWhenever you must find unknown support reactions or member forces before you can even start a strength-of-materials (deformation/failure) calculation — statics is always step one.
ExampleA civil engineer designing a truss bridge first uses Statics to find the force in each steel member (e.g. "member AB carries 25 kN tension"). Only afterward does Mechanics of Materials check whether a member of that size and steel grade can survive 25 kN without breaking.
Watch outStudents think Statics tells you whether something will fail. It doesn't — it only tells you the forces. Whether those forces cause failure is a materials question, answered in a later course.

What Can You Do with Statics Knowledge? / Statics Importance / Hyatt Walkway Collapse

PurposeTo motivate the course with a real consequence: statics errors kill people. The Hyatt Regency walkway collapse (Kansas City, 1981, 114 deaths) happened because a construction-stage design change silently doubled the load on a connection, and nobody re-ran the statics.
When to useWhenever a design changes mid-project (e.g. a contractor substitutes a different connection detail), re-run the free-body diagram and equilibrium equations — don't assume the original numbers still apply.
ExampleIn the original design, one long threaded rod carried both the 2nd- and 4th-floor walkways, so the connection at the 4th floor only had to support the 4th floor's own load. The as-built version used two separate rods, but routed the 4th-floor walkway's load through the SAME connection as the 2nd-floor walkway — meaning that single connection now had to carry both walkways' loads combined, roughly double what it was designed for. It failed.
Watch outThe lesson isn't "walkways are dangerous" — it's "always draw the free-body diagram for the AS-BUILT structure, not the as-designed one." A change that looks minor in a drawing can double a force in reality.

Basic Quantities Used in Mechanics / Fundamental Concept & Idealization

PurposeStatics needs four precisely defined building-block quantities (length, time, mass, force) plus two simplifying idealizations (particle, rigid body) so that every problem can be described with the same consistent vocabulary.
When to useTreat an object as a particle when its size/shape doesn't matter to the problem (e.g. a block sliding on ice, if you only care about its overall motion). Treat it as a rigid body when its shape and dimensions matter (e.g. finding support reactions on a beam, where the length between supports affects the moment).
ExampleA wrecking ball swinging on a cable: for finding the tension in the cable, model the ball as a particle (a point mass) — its 1 m diameter doesn't matter. A steel beam bolted at both ends: model it as a rigid body, because the 4 m distance between the bolts directly changes the moment calculation.
Watch outStudents forget that "rigid body" doesn't mean "unbreakable" — it's an idealization meaning deformation is small enough to ignore FOR THIS ANALYSIS, not that the object can never fail.

Newton's Laws (1st, then 2nd & 3rd)

PurposeNewton's First Law (∑F = 0 ⟹ v = constant) IS the mathematical definition of static equilibrium — it's the reason the entire "Statics" course exists as separate from "Dynamics." The Third Law (action = -reaction) is what lets you draw free-body diagrams of individual connected parts.
When to useUse the First Law any time you're asked to prove or use "the object is in equilibrium." Use the Third Law any time you separate a structure into pieces (e.g. a beam resting on a wall) — the force the beam pushes on the wall equals and opposes the force the wall pushes on the beam.
ExampleA book resting on a table: the book pushes down on the table with a force equal to its weight (say 20 N). By Newton's Third Law, the table pushes UP on the book with exactly 20 N. These two 20 N forces act on different objects (one on the table, one on the book) — they do not cancel each other; each one is balanced separately by other forces on its own object.
Watch outThe single most common mistake: students think the Third Law pair (action/reaction) cancels out on the SAME free-body diagram. It never does — action and reaction always act on two different bodies, so they never appear together in one object's equilibrium equation.

Newton's Law of Gravitation / System of Units

PurposeGives the formula for gravitational attraction (F = GMm/r²) and its everyday simplification for objects near Earth's surface (F = mg, g = 9.81 m/s²) — this is how "weight" gets computed from "mass" in every later problem. It also introduces the two competing unit systems (SI and US Customary/FPS) you'll see throughout the course.
When to useUse F = mg any time a problem gives you a mass in kg and you need the weight force in Newtons for a free-body diagram (which is almost every problem in this course).
ExampleA 1000 kg crane (from the page-109 problem later in the course) has weight W = mg = 1000 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 9810 N = 9.81 kN. That 9.81 kN is exactly the downward force you'd draw at the crane's center of gravity on its FBD.
Watch outStudents confuse mass (kg, a property of matter, same everywhere) with weight (N, a force, changes with local gravity). "This box weighs 5 kg" is technically wrong — 5 kg is its mass; its weight is 5 × 9.81 = 49.05 N.
pg 22–24

Mass & Weight / Mass and Weight (FPS vs SI) / Force Derived Units

PurposeTo pin down exactly how mass and weight convert into each other in the two unit systems you'll encounter (SI: mass is basic, weight is derived; FPS: weight/force is basic, mass is derived) and to define the derived force-based units (Pascal for stress, N·m for moment).
When to useUse W = m·g when working in SI (g = 9.81 m/s²); use m = W/g when working in FPS (g = 32.2 ft/s²), since in FPS the "pound" you're given is already a force (weight), and mass in slugs is what you compute.
ExampleSI: a 50 kg person has weight W = 50 × 9.81 = 490.5 N. FPS: a person weighing 110 lb has mass m = W/g = 110/32.2 = 3.42 slugs.
Watch outMixing the two systems mid-problem (e.g. using g = 9.81 with a mass given in slugs) gives numbers that are wrong by a large, easy-to-miss factor. Always check which system you're in before you plug in g.

Conversion of Units — SI Unit Prefixes / Rules for Use of Units & Prefixes

PurposePrefixes (kilo, milli, nano, etc.) let engineers write very large or very small numbers compactly, but only if used consistently — the rules exist to prevent silent factor-of-1000 errors when multiplying prefixed quantities together.
When to useAny time you multiply two quantities that each carry a metric prefix (like a force in kN times a distance in mm) — convert both to base units first, multiply, then re-express in a sensible prefix.
ExampleFrom the slide: (50 kN)(60 nm). Convert to base units first: 50 kN = 50 × 10³ N, and 60 nm = 60 × 10⁻⁹ m. Multiplying: (50 × 10³)(60 × 10⁻⁹) = 3000 × 10⁻⁶ N·m = 3 × 10³ × 10⁻⁶ N·m = 3 N·mm. Note the result is in N·mm, not N·m — you must track the exponents through the whole calculation, not just multiply the front numbers (50 × 60 = 3000) and guess the prefix.
Watch outNever use compound prefixes like "kμs" (kilo-micro-seconds) — always reduce to one prefix. Also, prefixes belong in the numerator of a unit, not the denominator, except for kg (which is the one base SI unit that already contains a prefix).

Example: Unit Conversions / What Happens If We Mix Units? (Mars Climate Orbiter)

PurposeTo teach the "multiply by 1" method for converting units safely, and to show via a real, catastrophic example (NASA's 1998 Mars Climate Orbiter, lost because one team used pound-force and another used Newtons) why unit consistency is not optional.
When to useAny time a problem statement gives you data in mixed units (e.g. some lengths in feet, others in meters) — convert everything to ONE consistent system before doing any arithmetic.
ExampleConvert a torque of 47 in·lb into SI (N·m), as on the slide. Write the conversion as fractions equal to 1, with the unit you want to cancel on the bottom: 47 in·lb × (0.0254 m / 1 in) × (4.448 N / 1 lb) = 47 × 0.0254 × 4.448 N·m = 5.31 N·m. Each fraction is a "disguised 1" (since 1 in really does equal 0.0254 m), so multiplying by it changes the number's units but not its physical size.
Watch outThe Mars Climate Orbiter didn't fail because of a math error — it failed because two teams (NASA using metric, Lockheed Martin's software outputting pound-force) each did their own math correctly but never agreed on units. The lesson: state your units explicitly on every number, every time, even when "everyone knows" what system you're using.

Problem-Solving Strategy (1) and (2)

PurposeGives the repeatable step-by-step recipe (sketch → FBD → label forces → choose axes → apply ∑F=0 and ∑M=0) that every statics problem in this course follows — memorizing the recipe, not just the formulas, is what actually lets you solve new problems.
When to useAt the start of literally every equilibrium problem you'll ever face in this course, before you write a single equation.
ExampleApplying the recipe to a hanging sign held by two cables: (1) sketch the sign, (2) draw the FBD showing only the two cable tension forces and the weight (not the wall or ceiling), (3) label knowns (weight = 100 N) and unknowns (T₁, T₂), (4) set up x-y axes, (5) resolve each tension into x and y components, (6) apply ∑Fx = 0 and ∑Fy = 0 to solve for T₁ and T₂.
Watch outSkipping the FBD and trying to write equations "in your head" is the single biggest source of sign errors in this course — always draw it first, even for problems that look simple.
pg 28–29

Problem-Solving Strategy (3) — sign conventions for moments

PurposeEstablishes a universal sign rule (counterclockwise = positive, clockwise = negative) so that everyone's moment equations are comparable, and gives the final "sanity check" step (verify units, check the FBD is self-consistent) that catches most errors before they become a wrong final answer.
When to useEvery time you sum moments about a point — decide your positive rotational direction (usually CCW) BEFORE you start plugging in numbers, and stick with it for every term in that equation.
ExampleIf you solve for a reaction force and get a negative number (say Ax = -50 N), that doesn't mean you made an arithmetic mistake — it means the force actually points opposite to the arrow direction you originally assumed on your FBD. The magnitude (50 N) is still correct; only the assumed sense was wrong.
Watch outStudents see a negative answer and assume they did something wrong, then "fix" it by flipping the sign — don't. A negative result is valid information (wrong assumed direction), not an error to erase.
pg 29

Dimensional Homogeneity / Method of Problem Solution (Summary)

PurposeDimensional homogeneity is a free error-checking tool: every term added together in a correct physics equation must have the same units, so checking units is a fast way to catch a wrong formula before you even plug in numbers.
When to useRight after you derive or recall any equation and before you substitute numbers — check that every additive term reduces to the same base unit.
ExampleCheck s = vt + ½at² for dimensional homogeneity. s is in meters. vt has units (m/s)(s) = m. ½at² has units (m/s²)(s²) = m. Both terms reduce to meters, matching s — the equation is dimensionally consistent (this doesn't prove the equation is physically correct, but it does prove it isn't obviously wrong).
Watch outDimensional homogeneity catches unit errors but NOT numerical errors — an equation can be dimensionally perfect and still have the wrong coefficient (e.g. writing s = 2vt + ½at² would still pass a units check but be physically wrong). Use it as one check among several, not a complete proof.

Introduction (Ch.2 roadmap) / Coordinate Systems

PurposeThis chapter's roadmap explains WHY we now treat bodies as more than single points: rigid bodies can translate AND rotate, so we need moments, couples, and a formal coordinate system to describe positions and directions unambiguously.
When to usePick a coordinate origin and axis directions at the very start of any 2D or 3D force problem, and never change them mid-problem — every position and force must be measured from the same fixed reference.
ExampleFor a beam problem, placing the origin at the left support (instead of, say, the beam's midpoint) usually makes every distance in the problem a simple positive number measured rightward — a small choice that avoids sign confusion for the rest of the solution.
Watch outSwitching origins partway through a problem (e.g. measuring one distance from the left support and another from the right support) is a common source of sign errors in moment calculations.

What is A Force? / Force System Terminology

PurposeA force is invisible — only its effect can be observed — so this slide gives the vocabulary (line of action, collinear, concurrent, coplanar, equivalent, transmissibility) needed to describe forces precisely in every later calculation.
When to useUse "concurrent" to check whether several forces can be added directly at one point (like forces on a bolt); use "coplanar" to know whether a problem is safely 2D or needs full 3D treatment.
ExampleThree cables all tied to the same ring are concurrent forces — they all pass through one point (the ring), so you can add them with simple vector addition at that single point, without needing to worry about where along each cable the force "starts."
Watch outDon't confuse "collinear" (same line) with "concurrent" (same point) — two forces can be concurrent (meeting at a point) without being collinear (pointing along the same line), and vice versa.

External / Internal Forces on Rigid Body / Principle of Transmissibility

PurposeSeparates forces into "external" (what you draw on a free-body diagram, and what determines whether the whole body moves) versus "internal" (what holds the body's own particles together, invisible on an external FBD but critical once you cut the body open to find member forces).
When to useUse the Principle of Transmissibility (a force can slide anywhere along its own line of action without changing the body's external behavior) to move a force to a more convenient point when computing a moment — but never use it if you actually need the INTERNAL force/stress at a specific location.
ExampleA rod is pulled by a 100 N force. If you push instead of pull along that same line, the rod's overall rigid-body motion is identical either way — but internally, pulling puts the rod in tension while pushing puts it in compression. Externally equivalent, internally very different.
Watch outStudents apply transmissibility when solving for INTERNAL forces (like the tension in a specific segment of a rope) — that's the one case where sliding the force along its line of action changes the real physical answer, even though the external equilibrium is unaffected.

Free Body Diagram (FBD) & Procedure

PurposeThe FBD isolates ONE body from everything touching it and replaces every other body/surface with the force it exerts — this is the single tool that turns a messy real-world picture into a solvable set of equations.
When to useAt the start of every single equilibrium problem, before writing any equation — no exceptions.
ExampleA block hangs from a rope from the ceiling (the deck's own sketch). The full "sketch" shows the ceiling, the rope, and the block. The FBD of just the block shows only two forces: its weight mg pointing down, and the rope tension T pointing up. The ceiling and rope material itself vanish from the diagram — they're represented only by the force (T) they exert on the block.
Watch outThe most common FBD mistake: including a force that belongs to the wrong body (e.g. drawing the weight of the ROPE on the block's FBD, or drawing the wall's reaction force when the wall isn't touching the object you isolated).
pg 36

Which FBD is Correct? (ladder example) / Trigonometry (Right Angle)

PurposeTests whether you can correctly identify and draw all three forces on a real object (a ladder against a wall: gravity, friction, normal force) — a skill test, not new theory. The trig recap exists because every 2D force problem needs the Pythagorean theorem to relate a triangle's sides.
When to useUse the ladder scenario as your mental checklist any time an object touches two different surfaces (ground AND wall): each surface contributes its own normal force, and friction can act at EITHER or BOTH contact points depending on which surfaces are rough.
ExampleA uniform ladder leans against a smooth (frictionless) wall and rests on rough ground (μs = 0.40). Because the wall is smooth, only a normal force acts there (no friction on the wall) — but the ground is rough, so BOTH a normal force AND a friction force act there. A common wrong FBD adds friction at the wall too, which is incorrect here because the wall was stated to be smooth.
Watch outAlways re-check the problem statement for words like "smooth" (= frictionless, normal force only) versus "rough" (friction possible) at EACH contact point separately — one surface can be smooth while another is rough in the same problem.
Diagram: FBD of a ladder against a smooth wall (pg 36)
The ladder leans at angle θ from the ground. The wall is smooth, so it can only push horizontally on the ladder (N_wall) — no friction there. The ground is rough, so it pushes both vertically (N_floor) and horizontally (F_friction, resisting the base's tendency to slide away from the wall). Weight W acts straight down at the ladder's midpoint (its center of gravity).

Trigonometry (Any Triangle)

PurposeDefines sine, cosine, and tangent as ratios of a right triangle's sides — this is the actual mechanism by which every force gets split into x and y components throughout the entire course.
When to useAny time you need to break one force at an angle into its horizontal and vertical pieces, or go the other way (recombine known x,y components back into a magnitude and angle).
ExampleA 100 N force acts at 30° above horizontal. Horizontal component = 100 cos(30°) = 86.6 N. Vertical component = 100 sin(30°) = 50 N. Check with Pythagoras: √(86.6² + 50²) = √(7500 + 2500) = √10000 = 100 N ✓ — matches the original magnitude, confirming the split was done correctly.
Watch outMixing up which side is "opposite" vs. "adjacent" relative to the angle you're using is the #1 source of swapped sin/cos errors — always re-derive it from the triangle sketch rather than from memory of "which formula goes with which axis."
pg 38–39

Scalar and Vector Quantities Comparison

PurposeSeparates quantities that need only a number (scalars: mass, time, speed, energy) from quantities that need a number AND a direction (vectors: force, velocity, displacement) — mixing these up leads to nonsensical equations like adding a force to a mass.
When to useWhenever you're about to "add" two physical quantities, first ask: do they have direction? If yes (vectors), you must add them by components or geometrically, not just by adding the numbers.
ExampleTwo people push the same box: one with 30 N to the right, another with 40 N upward. As scalars you might be tempted to say "70 N total" — wrong, because these are vectors at 90° to each other. The correct resultant magnitude is √(30² + 40²) = √(900+1600) = √2500 = 50 N, not 70 N.
Watch outWeight and velocity are commonly mis-remembered as scalars because their everyday use ("the box weighs 50 N," "the car goes 60 km/h") often omits direction — but formally, both are vectors (weight always points toward Earth's center; velocity has a direction of travel).

Vectors classification / Product (Multiply) a Vector

PurposeDistinguishes fixed, free, and sliding vectors because the rules for "can I move this vector without changing the answer" differ: a force is a sliding vector (transmissibility applies), a moment is a free vector (can act anywhere), but a position vector is fixed (tied to a specific point).
When to useBefore you move any vector on paper to simplify a diagram, first classify it — moving a fixed vector (like a position vector) changes the answer; moving a free vector (like a moment) does not.
ExampleMultiplying vector U (magnitude 5, pointing right) by scalar a = -2 gives -2U: magnitude |−2||U| = 2×5 = 10, but direction flips to point left (because a is negative).
Watch outForgetting that a negative scalar multiplier flips the vector's direction (not just shrinks/grows its magnitude) is a common error when manipulating vector equations.

Unit Vector (Specify Direction) / Cartesian Unit Vectors

PurposeA unit vector (magnitude exactly 1, no units) isolates "pure direction" from "size" — this lets you write ANY vector as (its magnitude) × (a direction), which is the standard form used for every force in 3D problems later in the course.
When to useWhenever a problem gives you a force's magnitude and a line of action (e.g. "a 500 N force acts along the cable from A to B") but not directly its x,y,z components — you build the unit vector along that line first, then multiply by the magnitude.
ExampleVector A has magnitude 10 and points in the direction (3, 4) (unnormalized). |A| = √(3²+4²) = 5. The unit vector is uA = A/|A| = (3/5, 4/5) = (0.6, 0.8) — check: |uA| = √(0.36+0.64) = √1 = 1 ✓. The actual vector A (magnitude 10, same direction) is then 10 × (0.6, 0.8) = (6, 8).
Watch outDividing by the WRONG magnitude (e.g. dividing by the target vector's magnitude instead of the direction-defining vector's own length) is a common slip — always compute |vector| from that same vector's own components before dividing.
pg 42

Position Vector in Two Dimensions / Vector Addition (intro)

PurposeGives the "head minus tail" rule for building a position vector between two known points — this single rule is reused constantly, from computing moments to finding cable directions in 3D.
When to useAny time you're given the coordinates of two points and need the vector pointing from one to the other (e.g. from a pin at A to a point on a cable at B).
ExamplePoint A is at (2, 1) and point B is at (5, 4). rAB = (xB−xA)i + (yB−yA)j = (5−2)i + (4−1)j = 3i + 3j. This means: to walk from A to B, go 3 units right and 3 units up.
Watch outReversing the subtraction order (computing rBA when the problem needs rAB) flips the vector's direction by 180° — always double check which point is the "tail" (subtracted) and which is the "head" (first).
pg 43–44

Vector Addition (Parallelogram Law) / Vector Addition (Triangle Law)

PurposeThese are the two equivalent geometric ways to add two vectors: parallelogram law (both vectors from the same tail, resultant is the diagonal) and triangle law (place head-to-tail, resultant closes the triangle). Both exist because different problems are easier to visualize one way or the other.
When to useUse the triangle law when chaining several displacements/forces in sequence (like a hiking path); use the parallelogram law when two forces both originate from the same point (like two cables pulling on the same hook) and you want to visualize their combined effect as one diagonal force.
ExampleIf a book is displaced by vector U, then displaced again by vector V, the triangle law says: draw U, then draw V starting from U's head; the resultant W = U + V is the vector from U's tail directly to V's head. Vector addition is commutative — U + V gives the same final position as V + U, even though the intermediate path looks different.
Watch outIn the triangle law, students often connect head-to-head or tail-to-tail instead of head-to-tail — the second vector's TAIL must start exactly at the first vector's HEAD, or the resultant will be wrong.
Diagram: Triangle Law of vector addition (pg 43–44)
Draw vector U first. Then draw vector V starting exactly at U's head (tip-to-tail). The resultant W = U + V is the single vector drawn from U's original tail straight to V's final head — it "closes" the triangle. This works for any two vectors, in any order.

Parallelogram Law (N Vectors) / Vector Subtraction / Example 1: Vector Operations (Graphical)

PurposeExtends addition to many vectors at once (chain triangle law repeatedly) and shows subtraction as "addition of the negative vector" — plus a graphical (ruler-and-protractor) method as a quick sanity check on analytical answers.
When to useUse the graphical method as a fast estimate/check when you don't trust your analytical (trigonometric) answer, or when a rough sketch is all that's needed.
ExampleGiven |U| = 8 (horizontal) and |V| = 3 (vertical), find U + 2V graphically. First scale 2V to magnitude 6 (still vertical). Draw U (8 units) then, starting from U's head, draw 2V (6 units, vertical). The resultant is the vector from U's tail to 2V's head — by Pythagoras this should measure √(8² + 6²) = √(64+36) = √100 = 10 units, which you'd confirm by direct ruler measurement on the scaled drawing.
Watch outGraphical solutions are only as accurate as your drawing — treat them as an estimate/check, not a final answer, whenever the problem expects precision (this course generally wants the trigonometric/analytical answer).

Addition of Vectors: Resultant of Two Forces — Example 2 (Problem 2.1)

PurposeDemonstrates that when two forces are known only by magnitude and angle (not components), the Law of Cosines and Law of Sines let you find the resultant's magnitude and direction directly, without first breaking into x,y components.
When to useUse this method when exactly two forces are involved and you're given magnitudes plus the angle between them — for three or more forces, component addition (covered next) is faster.
ExampleTwo forces P = 40 N and Q = 60 N act on a bolt, with the angle between them (measured as the interior angle of the force triangle) equal to 155°. Law of Cosines: R² = P² + Q² − 2PQ cos(B), where B = 155°. R² = 40² + 60² − 2(40)(60)cos(155°) = 1600 + 3600 − 4800(−0.906) = 5200 + 4349 = 9549, so R = √9549 ≈ 97.73 N. Law of Sines then gives the angle A INSIDE the triangle, between R and force Q: sin(A)/Q = sin(B)/R → sin(A) = 60×sin(155°)/97.73 ≈ 0.2593, so A ≈ 15.04°. This 15.04° is only the angle inside the constructed triangle, not yet the final answer — force P itself was drawn at 20° above the horizontal, so the resultant's true direction above the horizontal is α = 20° + 15.04° = 35.04°. Final answer: R = 97.73 N, directed at α ≈ 35.04° from the horizontal (matches the deck's boxed answer).
Watch outTwo separate mistakes are easy to make here. First, the angle you plug into the Law of Cosines is the angle INSIDE the constructed triangle (often 180° minus the angle between the two original force vectors as drawn), not the raw angle between the forces. Second — and easier to miss — the angle the Law of Sines gives you (15.04° here) is only the angle inside the triangle; you still have to add back whatever angle the reference force (P) itself makes with the horizontal to get the resultant's true direction. Stopping at the triangle angle looks like a finished answer but is one step short.

Example 3: Vector Operations (Problem 2.2) — barge and tugboats

PurposeA real engineering application of the Triangle Law with Law of Sines: given the DESIRED resultant (a barge must move straight down its own axis) and the directions of two ropes, find the individual rope tensions needed to produce exactly that resultant.
When to useUse this approach any time you know the target resultant direction and magnitude and the LINES OF ACTION of the individual forces, but not yet their magnitudes — e.g. designing how hard two tugboats must pull.
ExampleThe resultant of two tugboat ropes must be 5 kN along the barge's axis. Components of a Force / Rectangular Components of a Force / Resolving A Force

PurposeIntroduces the component method: instead of geometric triangle constructions, break every force into perpendicular (usually x and y) pieces — this scales to any number of forces and is the method used for the rest of the course.
When to useWhenever three or more concurrent forces must be combined, or whenever a force at an angle needs to be split for use in separate ∑Fx = 0 and ∑Fy = 0 equations.
ExampleA force F = 200 N acts at θ = 40° above the horizontal. Fx = F cos θ = 200 × cos(40°) = 153.2 N. Fy = F sin θ = 200 × sin(40°) = 128.6 N. Check: θ = tan⁻¹(Fy/Fx) = tan⁻¹(128.6/153.2) = tan⁻¹(0.8396) = 40.0° ✓ recovers the original angle.
Watch outAlways measure the angle from the SAME reference axis stated in the problem (e.g. "from the horizontal" vs "from the vertical") — swapping which one you use silently swaps sin and cos in your component formulas.

Addition of Forces by Summing X and Y Components

PurposeFormalizes the "resolve everything, then add column-by-column" method for combining any number of concurrent forces — this is the workhorse technique for all 2D equilibrium problems in the course.
When to useWhenever you have three or more forces to combine, or need to solve for equilibrium of a particle under several forces — build a table of each force's x- and y-components, sum each column separately.
Example(This sets up the table method used explicitly in the next card's Example 4.) Watch outEvery force needs its OWN correct sign in each column based on which way it actually points (not just "always positive") — a force pointing left contributes a negative x-component even if its magnitude is written as a positive number.

Equilibrium of a Particle

PurposeDefines exactly what "in equilibrium" means for a particle (a body idealized as a single point): the resultant of ALL forces acting on it is zero. This single condition, ∑F = 0 (equivalently ∑Fx = 0 and ∑Fy = 0), is the entire toolkit for this chapter.
When to useUse particle equilibrium (not the full rigid-body version with moments) whenever every force in the problem passes through one common point — e.g. forces at a single pin, ring, or knot — because in that case moments about that point are automatically zero and add no new information.
ExampleTwo ropes and a weight all meet at one ring. Because every force passes through the ring, you only need ∑Fx = 0 and ∑Fy = 0 (two equations) to solve for up to two unknowns — you do NOT need a separate moment equation here, unlike a rigid body.
Watch outStudents sometimes add an unnecessary moment equation for a particle problem — it's not wrong, but it always gives 0 = 0 (no new information) since every force passes through the point, so it wastes time without helping solve for the unknowns.

Free-Body Diagram (FBD) — Space Diagram vs FBD / Tension & Compression / Pulleys

PurposeSeparates the "space diagram" (a realistic sketch of the whole physical setup) from the "free-body diagram" (only the forces on the ONE particle you isolated) — and establishes that ropes/cables/chains can only ever pull (tension), never push, while rigid bars can do either.
When to useUse the tension-only rule to immediately rule out impossible FBD arrows — if a "solution" requires a rope to push, that assumed direction is physically wrong and must be flipped, or the structure would go slack instead.
ExampleA pulley redirects a rope's tension without changing its magnitude (frictionless, massless pulley assumption): if a rope has 100 lb of tension on one side of a pulley, it still has 100 lb of tension on the other side, just pointing a different direction — this is why pulleys are useful for changing the DIRECTION of a pull without a mechanical-advantage machine.
Watch outAssuming a pulley changes the MAGNITUDE of tension (like a lever would) — a single frictionless pulley never does this; it only redirects the force. (Multiple pulleys in a block-and-tackle system CAN change magnitude, but that's a different, more advanced setup not covered on this slide.)

Example 5: Force Equilibrium (Problem 2.4) — automobile on a cable

PurposeShows the standard 3-force particle equilibrium method using the Law of Sines directly on the closed force polygon (triangle), an alternative to resolving into x,y components when only three forces are involved.
When to useUse the force-triangle + Law of Sines method when exactly three forces act at a point and all three angles between them are known — it's often faster than full component resolution for a 3-force particle problem.
ExampleA 3.5 kN automobile is supported by cable AC, with a rope AB pulling sideways. The force triangle has interior angles of 120° and 2° (from the geometry given) leaving the third angle at 58°. Sanity-check every answer against the triangle sketch: the side opposite the LARGEST angle (here, 120°, opposite side AB) must get the LARGEST value — since TAB = 3.57 kN is indeed larger than TAC = 144 N here, that confirms the pairing is right.

Components in Three Dimensions (Position Vector) — Example 7

Rigid Bodies: Equivalent Systems of Forces / External & Internal Forces / Principle of Transmissibility

PurposeOnce we can no longer treat a body as a single point, we need new tools — the moment of a force — to describe its tendency to ROTATE a rigid body, not just push it in a straight line. This chapter builds those tools.
When to useRecognize you need this chapter's tools whenever a force is applied somewhere OTHER than the body's exact center — any offset force creates both a translation tendency and a rotation tendency.
ExamplePushing a door exactly at its hinge produces zero rotation (the moment arm is zero) no matter how hard you push. Pushing the same force at the door's outer edge (a large moment arm) swings it open easily — same force, very different rotational effect, purely because of WHERE it's applied.
Watch outPrinciple of transmissibility says you can slide F along its own line of action without changing the body's external motion — but you cannot move F to a DIFFERENT (parallel) line without adding a compensating couple, which is exactly the topic of the "Movement of a Force" cards later in this chapter.

Vector/Cross Product of Two Vectors (2D) / Principle of Moments

PurposeThe cross product (V = P × Q, magnitude PQ sinθ, direction by right-hand rule) is the mathematical machine that computes a moment: it naturally produces a result perpendicular to both the position vector and the force, matching how a moment (rotation axis) behaves physically.
When to useUse the cross product whenever you need a moment in full 3D (where "clockwise vs counterclockwise" isn't enough — you need an actual axis direction). For pure 2D problems, the simpler scalar method (M = Fd) usually suffices.
ExampleP = 3i and Q = 4j (perpendicular vectors, magnitudes 3 and 4). |V| = |P||Q|sin(90°) = 3×4×1 = 12. Direction: curl your right-hand fingers from P (pointing +x) toward Q (pointing +y) — your thumb points in +z. So P × Q = 12k.
Watch outThe cross product is NOT commutative: P × Q = −(Q × P). Swapping the order flips the sign — always keep track of which vector comes first in r × F (position vector always comes first, force second).

Moment About a Point (Vector (3D) & Scalar (2D) Method)

PurposeDefines the moment of a force about a point as MO = r × F (vector method, works in 2D and 3D) or the equivalent scalar shortcut MO = Fd, where d is the PERPENDICULAR (shortest) distance from the point to the force's line of action.
When to useUse MO = Fd (scalar) for quick 2D hand calculations when the perpendicular distance is easy to see or compute geometrically; use MO = r × F (vector) for 3D problems or when the perpendicular distance is awkward to find directly.
ExampleA 100 N force acts straight down on the end of a horizontal wrench, 0.3 m from the bolt (pivot O). Since the force is perpendicular to the wrench, d = 0.3 m exactly, so MO = Fd = 100 × 0.3 = 30 N·m, tending to rotate the wrench clockwise (negative by the standard CCW-positive convention).
Watch outd must be the PERPENDICULAR distance from O to the force's LINE OF ACTION (extended infinitely in both directions if needed) — not simply the straight-line distance from O to wherever the force happens to be applied on the body. Using the wrong distance is the single most common moment-calculation error.

Moment of a Force About a Point (2D: Scalar Method) / Geometrical Interpretation / Right-Hand Rule / Varignon's Theorem

PurposeFormalizes the CCW = positive sign convention, shows geometrically that the moment magnitude equals the area of the parallelogram formed by r and F (base × height), and states Varignon's Theorem: the moment of a force's resultant equals the sum of moments of its individual components — letting you compute a moment component-by-component instead of needing the full perpendicular distance to the whole force.
When to useUse Varignon's Theorem whenever finding the true perpendicular distance to a force directly would require awkward geometry — instead, resolve the force into x and y components first, then sum the (easier) moments of each component separately.
ExampleA 100 N force acts at 30° above horizontal, applied at a point 2 m to the right and 0 m up from pivot O (i.e., position vector r = 2i m). Instead of finding the perpendicular distance to the tilted force directly, split F into Fx = 100cos30° = 86.6 N and Fy = 100sin30° = 50 N. Moment of Fx about O: since Fx is horizontal and r is also horizontal (r has no y-component), Fx produces zero moment (d=0 for that component). Moment of Fy about O: MO = r × Fy = 2 m × 50 N = 100 N·m (CCW, since Fy points up and acts to the right of O). Total MO = 0 + 100 = 100 N·m — found without ever computing the perpendicular distance to the original tilted 100 N force.
Watch outThe right-hand rule sign convention (CCW = out of the page = positive) is a CHOICE you must state and then apply consistently for the whole problem — some textbooks/instructors define clockwise as positive instead, so always check which convention a given problem is using.

Example 1: Force Moment about a Point (Problem 3.1) — lever on a shaft

PurposeShows FIVE different ways to produce the exact same moment about a point (vary the force's magnitude, direction, or point of application) — proving that a moment only depends on Force × perpendicular distance, not on any one specific configuration.
When to useUse this insight when DESIGNING a mechanism: if you need a specific moment but are constrained in how/where you can apply force (e.g. limited space), you can trade off force magnitude against distance, or angle against distance, to still hit the target moment.
ExampleA 500 N vertical force acts at point A, 0.6 m from pivot O, with the lever oriented 60° from the force's line. (a) MO = F×d = 500 × (0.6×cos60°) = 500 × 0.3 = 150 N·m (clockwise, so −150 N·m by CCW-positive convention). (b) A horizontal force at A producing the same 150 N·m: here d = 0.6×sin60° = 0.52 m, so F = 150/0.52 ≈ 288.5 N. (c) The SMALLEST possible force at A: occurs when F is perpendicular to OA (maximizing d = 0.6 m, the full length), giving F = 150/0.6 = 250 N. (d) Where must a 1.2 kN vertical force be applied to give the same moment? d = 150/1200 = 0.125 m (perpendicular distance), so along the lever direction, OB = 0.125/cos60° = 0.25 m.
Watch outPart (e) of this problem asks: are any of these equivalent to the ORIGINAL 500 N force? No — equivalence requires the SAME magnitude, direction, AND line of action, not just the same moment. Producing the same moment is a much weaker condition than being an equivalent force.

Example 2 & 3: Force Moment about a Point — simple lever/beam balance

PurposeApplies the moment-balance idea (sum of moments about a pivot = 0) to a classic lever/seesaw-style beam, including the more realistic case where the beam's own weight also contributes a moment.
When to useUse "take moments about the pivot/support point" as your go-to first move whenever a beam or lever is held by one pin/pivot and you need to find an unknown force — choosing the pivot as your moment center makes the pivot's own reaction force disappear from that equation (since its moment arm is zero).

Vector (Cross) Product of Unit Vectors (3D) / Determinant Method

PurposeGives the determinant shortcut for computing r × F in 3D — instead of manually applying i×i=0, i×j=k, etc. term by term, you can set up a 3×3 determinant and expand it mechanically, which is far less error-prone for full 3D problems.
When to useUse the determinant method for essentially every 3D moment calculation from this point in the course onward — it's the standard, systematic approach used in every subsequent 3D example.
ExampleFind i × j using the base rule: i×j = k (by definition/right-hand rule), while i×i = 0 (a vector crossed with itself is always zero, since sinθ=0 when θ=0). Watch outThe determinant expansion has an alternating sign pattern (+, −, +) for the i, j, k terms respectively — forgetting the MINUS sign on the middle (j) term is a very common algebra slip.

Scalar (Dot) Product of Two Vectors / Mixed Triple Product

PurposeThe dot product (P · Q = |P||Q|cosθ, a SCALAR result) exists to find angles between vectors and to project one vector onto an axis — this is different from and complementary to the cross product, which gives a perpendicular vector.
When to useUse the dot product when you need the angle between two vectors, or the component of a force ALONG a specific line/axis (like finding how much of a cable's tension acts along a particular beam's axis).
ExampleFind the angle between P = 3i+4j (|P|=5) and Q = 1i+0j (|Q|=1). P·Q = (3)(1)+(4)(0) = 3. cosθ = (P·Q)/(|P||Q|) = 3/(5×1) = 0.6, so θ = cos⁻¹(0.6) ≈ 53.1°.
Watch outUnlike the cross product, the dot product IS commutative (P·Q = Q·P) — but the RESULT is a plain scalar (no i, j, k units attached), so accidentally writing a unit vector on a dot-product answer is a sign you've made an error somewhere.

Moment (Magnitude) of a Force About a Given Axis — Example 5 (Problem 3.5, cube)

PurposeShows how to find the moment about a specific AXIS (like edge AB of a cube), not just about a point — you compute MO about a point on the axis first, then project that moment vector onto the axis using the dot product. This isolates only the twisting effect that matters for rotation specifically about that axis, discarding twisting about other directions.
When to useUse "moment about an axis" (not just about a point) whenever the real physical question is about rotation around one specific fixed axis, like a door swinging on its hinge line or a shaft twisting inside its bearings — the moment about a POINT includes rotation tendencies the axis can't actually experience (since the axis constrains motion to just one direction).
ExampleFor a cube of side a, using the deck's own setup: rF/A = a(i−j), and force P = P√2(i+j). (a) MA = rF/A × P = a(i−j) × P√2(i+j), which the deck evaluates (via the full determinant, using F's true 3D position on the cube) to the boxed result MA = aP√2 (i+j+k). (b) Moment about edge AB is the projection of MA onto unit vector i (since AB runs along the x-axis): MAB = i·MA = i·[aP√2(i+j+k)] = aP√2. (c) Moment about body diagonal AG: unit vector λ = (1/√3)(i−j−k), so MAG = λ·MA = (1/√3)(i−j−k)·(aP/√2)(i+j+k) = (aP/√6)(1−1−1) = −aP/√6 (boxed answer). (d) Since P·λ = 0 here, P turns out to be exactly perpendicular to diagonal AG, so |MAG| = Pd gives the perpendicular distance between skew lines AG and FC directly: d = a/√6.
Watch outThe moment about an axis is generally SMALLER than the moment about a point on that axis — because projecting onto one direction (dot product) discards the components of rotation that aren't aligned with that axis. Reporting the point-moment's full magnitude when the question asked for the axis-moment is a common mistake.

Moment of a Couple

PurposeA couple (two equal, opposite, parallel, non-collinear forces) produces PURE rotation with zero net force — this matters because a couple's moment is the same about EVERY point in space (unlike a single force's moment, which depends on which point you choose), making couples a uniquely simple way to represent "pure twist."
When to useRecognize a couple whenever two equal-and-opposite forces act on a body without a common line of action — like both hands turning a steering wheel, or a lug wrench loosening a wheel nut (one hand pushes, the other pulls, on opposite sides of the wrench).
ExampleTwo 50 N forces, opposite in direction, act on opposite ends of a 0.4 m wrench (perpendicular distance between their lines of action = 0.4 m). Moment of the couple M = F×d = 50 × 0.4 = 20 N·m. This 20 N·m value is the SAME no matter which point in space you choose to measure it about — try any point, and the two forces' individual moments will always sum to 20 N·m.
Watch outBecause a couple's net FORCE is always zero, students sometimes assume it has "no effect" — it absolutely does: pure rotation without translation. Never discard a couple just because ∑F = 0 for it.

Movement of a Force in Rigid Body (Case I & II) — force-couple system

PurposeShows exactly how to legally move a force to a DIFFERENT point O (not just slide it along its own line): you can do it, but only if you simultaneously ADD a compensating couple MO = r × F equal to the moment the force used to produce about O. This "force-couple system" is what lets engineers replace a complicated force layout with one simple equivalent force plus one moment.
When to useUse a force-couple system any time you want to replace several real forces acting at different points with ONE simplified equivalent system at a single convenient point — extremely common when finding the net effect of distributed loads or multiple bolts/rivets.
ExampleA 200 N force acts 3 m to the right of point O (purely vertical, downward). To move this force TO point O, you must add a couple equal to the moment the force used to have about O: M = F×d = 200×3 = 600 N·m (clockwise). The new "force-couple system" at O is: a 200 N downward force PLUS a 600 N·m clockwise couple — together, this produces the exact same effect on the rigid body as the original single offset force.
Watch outMoving a force-couple system from O to a further point O′ requires adding ANOTHER moment term (the moment of the resultant force R about O′) — each time you shift the reference point, you must re-add a new correction couple; the couple doesn't just "carry over" unchanged.

System of Forces: Reduction to a Force and Couple / Further Reduction

PurposeGeneralizes the force-couple idea to an ENTIRE system of forces at once: any collection of forces on a rigid body reduces to one resultant force R at a chosen point O, plus one resultant couple MO. If R and MO end up perpendicular (which happens automatically when forces are concurrent, coplanar, or parallel), the whole system can be further reduced to a SINGLE equivalent force along a shifted line of action.
When to useUse the "further reduction to a single force" simplification specifically for coplanar force systems (all forces in one plane) — this is the most common case in this course (2D beam and truss problems) and it always allows a single-force reduction.
ExampleA coplanar system reduces to R (known) and MO (known, both perpendicular by virtue of being coplanar). The single equivalent force's new line of action is offset from O by a perpendicular distance found by rearranging MO = R × d → d = MO/R — for instance, if MO = 500 N·m and R = 100 N, the equivalent single force is still 100 N but shifted d = 500/100 = 5 m away from O along the direction that reproduces the original moment.
Watch outThis single-force reduction ONLY works cleanly when R and MO are perpendicular — for a general 3D (non-coplanar, non-concurrent, non-parallel) force system, you generally CANNOT reduce everything to one single force; the true minimum is a "wrench" (a force plus a couple acting along the SAME line), which is beyond this course's scope but explains why the slide specifically calls out coplanar/concurrent/parallel as the special cases that work.

Example 7: Force Moment (Problem 3.8) — beam force-couple reduction

PurposeA complete worked example of reducing several parallel forces on a beam to (a) an equivalent force-couple at A, (b) the same system re-expressed at a different point B, and (c) a single equivalent resultant force at yet another location — demonstrating all the ideas from the two previous cards in one problem.
When to useThis exact technique is how engineers replace multiple point loads on a beam (from several concentrated weights or connections) with one simple equivalent load, before running the beam's own separate equilibrium/support-reaction analysis.
ExampleForces of 150 N, 600 N, 100 N, 250 N (all downward, −j) act at various points along the beam. Static and Dynamic Equilibrium / Static Equilibrium

PurposeExtends particle equilibrium (∑F=0 only) to full RIGID BODY equilibrium, which needs a second, independent condition: ∑M=0 (no net rotation). A rigid body can satisfy ∑F=0 (no net push) while still spinning up under an unbalanced couple — the moment equation catches that case.
When to useUse BOTH ∑F=0 AND ∑M=0 (about any point) any time forces do NOT all pass through one common point — which is true for essentially every rigid-body problem (beams, frames, trusses) from this point in the course onward.
ExampleA light rod has two equal, opposite, parallel forces (a couple) applied at its two ends, perpendicular to the rod. ∑F = F + (−F) = 0 (force equilibrium IS satisfied), but ∑M ≠ 0 about any point not on the couple's own line (the two forces both contribute a moment in the SAME rotational sense) — so the rod is in force equilibrium but NOT in moment/torque equilibrium; it will spin.
Watch outChecking only ∑F=0 and declaring a rigid body "in equilibrium" is the single most common mistake transitioning from Ch.3 (particles) to Ch.5 (rigid bodies) — a rigid body needs BOTH conditions checked, not just one.
Slide question → answer (pg 99) "Consider a light rod subject to the two forces of equal magnitude as shown. Choose the correct statement: (1) force eq. but not torque eq., (2) torque eq. but not force eq., (3) both, (4) neither."

The two equal-and-opposite forces cancel as a net force (∑F=0), but because they act along different (parallel, offset) lines, they form a couple that produces a net moment (∑M≠0), so the rod is NOT in rotational equilibrium.

Reactions @ Supports & Connections (2D) / Beam (Structural Element) / Support Connections

PurposeCatalogs the standard 2D support types (roller, pin, fixed) and states the core rule: a support provides a reaction force ONLY in the direction it physically restrains — this is what tells you how many unknowns each support contributes to your equilibrium equations.
When to useIdentify each support type FIRST when drawing a beam's FBD: a roller gives 1 unknown (perpendicular to the rolling surface only), a pin gives 2 unknowns (both x and y, but no moment — it can still rotate), and a fixed joint gives 3 unknowns (x, y, AND a moment, since it prevents all motion including rotation).
ExampleA diving board is bolted rigidly into a rigid deck at one end (fixed support) and unsupported at the other (free end). At the fixed end: 3 unknowns (Ax, Ay, MA). Watch outA pin support does NOT provide a moment reaction (it allows free rotation), even though it looks "solid" — only a FIXED (welded/embedded) support restrains rotation and thus contributes a moment unknown.

Equilibrium of a Rigid Body in Two Dimensions (2D)

PurposeStates the three independent scalar equations available for any 2D rigid body (∑Fx=0, ∑Fy=0, ∑MA=0 about any point A) and the crucial limit: exactly 3 unknowns can be solved for, no more — this number tells you immediately whether a structure is solvable with statics alone (determinate) or not (indeterminate).
When to useBefore starting ANY 2D rigid-body problem, count your unknown reaction components — if there are more than 3, the structure is statically INDETERMINATE and cannot be solved by these three equations alone (you'd need Mechanics of Materials/deformation equations too).
ExampleA beam has one pin support (2 unknowns: Ax, Ay) and one roller support (1 unknown: By). Total = 3 unknowns, exactly matching the 3 available equations — this beam is statically determinate and fully solvable with ∑Fx=0, ∑Fy=0, ∑MA=0.

Equilibrium of a Two-Force Body / Equilibrium of a Three-Force Body

PurposeProves two extremely useful shortcuts: (1) a rigid body in equilibrium under exactly TWO forces must have those forces equal, opposite, and COLLINEAR (same line of action) — no moment/trig calculation needed; (2) a body under exactly THREE forces in equilibrium must have all three forces either meeting at one common point (concurrent) or all parallel — this instantly tells you an unknown force's LINE OF ACTION before you even write an equation.
When to useWhenever a rigid body (like a single truss member, or a simple bracket) has exactly two or three forces acting on it, use these shortcuts to determine force directions geometrically FIRST — this often eliminates the need for a full moment equation entirely.
ExampleA simple strut is pinned at both ends with no load in between except the two pin reactions (a genuine two-force member). By the two-force-body rule, the reaction at each pin MUST lie exactly along the line connecting the two pins — you don't need to solve any equilibrium equations to know the reaction's direction, only its magnitude and sense (tension or compression) remain unknown. This exact insight is the entire foundation of the Method of Joints used throughout the truss chapter that follows.
Watch outThe two-force-body rule requires EXACTLY two forces (no applied loads along the member's length) — a member with a weight or a side-load applied mid-span is NOT a two-force member even if it only has two supports, because the member's own self-weight counts as a third force.

Centre of Gravity (Introduction) / Centroid

PurposeReal objects have weight spread over their whole volume, not concentrated at one point — the center of gravity exists so you can replace that whole spread-out weight with ONE single equivalent force at ONE point, dramatically simplifying every equilibrium equation. The centroid is the purely geometric version (the "center of area"), used when density is uniform.
When to useUse the centroid (geometry only, no weight needed) any time you need the center of a shape for computing distributed-load resultants or structural symmetry — use full center-of-gravity math only when density varies across the object.
ExampleA heavy filing cabinet standing upright: if you tip it, it will fall over once its center of gravity's vertical line moves outside its base footprint — this is exactly why movers are told to tip heavy furniture as little as possible, and why top-heavy objects (high center of gravity, narrow base) tip over more easily than short wide ones.
Watch outThe centroid does NOT have to be located inside the physical object — for an L-shaped or U-shaped bracket, the geometric centroid can fall in the empty space between the arms, which surprises students expecting it to always be "inside the material."

Center of Gravity (2D Body) / Common shapes (rectangle, triangle, circle)

PurposeGives the formal integral definitions (x̄ = ΣxA/ΣA, ȳ = ΣyA/ΣA, or the integral form for continuous shapes) plus memorized locations for the most common shapes, so you rarely need to integrate from scratch — a rectangle/square's centroid is at its geometric center, a triangle's is 1/3 of the height up from its base, and a circle/circular arc's is at its center.
When to useMemorize the triangle's "1/3 from the base" rule specifically — it's the most commonly mis-remembered (students often guess the midpoint, i.e. 1/2, by analogy with a rectangle).
ExampleA right triangle has its base on the x-axis with height 6 m. Its centroid's y-coordinate is 6 × (1/3) = 2 m above the base — NOT 3 m (which would be the midpoint, the common wrong guess).
Watch outThe "1/3 from the base" rule for a triangle is measured from the base to the OPPOSITE VERTEX along the height — always double check which side of the triangle is being treated as the "base" in a given problem, since the answer changes depending on that choice.

When to useALWAYS check for symmetry first before doing any integration or composite-shape calculation — it can immediately fix one or both centroid coordinates without any computation at all.
ExampleA capital "I" shape (like an I-beam cross-section) is symmetric about both a vertical AND a horizontal axis through its center. Composite Bodies / Centre of Gravity of Composite Bodies (2D) — the 3-step method

PurposeReal structural cross-sections are rarely one simple shape — they're combinations of rectangles, triangles, and circles (sometimes with holes). This slide gives the standard 3-step composite method: (1) split into simple shapes with known individual centroids, (2) find each part's area and centroid location, (3) take moments about a convenient axis to find the WHOLE shape's centroid — treating holes as NEGATIVE area.
When to useUse this exact method for essentially every real cross-section in this course (L-beams, C-channels, plates with bolt holes) — it's the standard, always-applicable technique.
ExampleA square plate with a circular hole cut from it: treat the WHOLE square as one positive-area piece, and the circular hole as a NEGATIVE-area piece at its own location — then apply X̄ = Σ(xᵢAᵢ)/ΣAᵢ using the hole's area as a negative number. This correctly "removes" the hole's contribution, shifting the composite centroid away from the hole (since material near the hole is now absent).
Watch outForgetting to make a hole's area NEGATIVE (instead treating it as if it were extra solid material) is the single most common composite-centroid mistake — always double-check the sign of every subtracted region.

When to useUse this every time a beam problem shows a distributed load (drawn as a triangle, rectangle, or trapezoid of arrows) instead of a single concentrated force arrow — first convert it to one equivalent point load before doing any equilibrium equations.

Example 6: Load Distribution (Problem 5.80) — Pore Water Pressure

PurposeApplies the same "distributed load → equivalent concentrated force at the centroid" idea to a real geotechnical/civil engineering scenario: hydrostatic (water) pressure increases linearly with depth below the water table, forming a TRIANGULAR pressure distribution, exactly like the beam-load case but now horizontal (pushing on a wall or dam).

Introduction / Internal Forces in Members / Tension & Compression

PurposeEstablishes the three categories of multi-part structures (trusses = all two-force members; frames = at least one multi-force member; machines = structures with moving parts) and defines tension (force pulls the member's ends outward, elongating it) vs. compression (force pushes ends inward, shortening it) — this distinction is fundamental to reading truss diagrams and to material selection (cables/ropes can only take tension; rigid bars can take either).
When to useClassify a structure into truss/frame/machine FIRST, since each category has a different standard solution method (Method of Joints/Sections for trusses; dismember-and-FBD-each-part for frames and machines).
ExampleA 100 lb force pulls the two ends of a bar apart: the bar is in tension, and it tends to elongate slightly (even if imperceptibly for a rigid-body idealization). The same 100 lb force pushing the two ends together puts the bar in compression, tending to shorten it — same magnitude, opposite physical effect.
Watch outA cable/rope/chain can ONLY be in tension — if your equilibrium solution gives a NEGATIVE tension for a cable, that's a sign the cable would need to push (impossible), meaning your original assumed structure/loading is physically inconsistent, not just a sign to flip.

Definition of a Truss / Trusses / Truss Types

PurposeA truss is defined by strict simplifying assumptions (straight two-force members, pin-connected joints, loads ONLY at joints, member weight split equally to adjacent nodes) — these assumptions are what make the whole Method of Joints/Sections mathematically possible; without them, every member would need full multi-force treatment.
When to useVerify a real structure actually satisfies the truss assumptions before applying truss methods — if a load is applied mid-span on a member (not at a joint), that member is no longer a pure two-force member and truss methods don't directly apply to it.
ExampleA bridge's steel roof truss has members bolted at each end to gusset plates (a rigid connection, technically not a frictionless pin) — but as long as loads are applied only at the joints and the members are slender/straight, engineers still model these bolted connections AS pins, because the resulting error is small and the simplification makes the analysis tractable.
Watch out"Stringers" (secondary beams supporting a deck/floor) are NOT truss members even in a bridge that's mostly a truss — they carry loads between joints, not along a two-force member path, so don't accidentally include them in a Method of Joints analysis.

Simple Trusses / Method of Joints (introduction)

PurposeThe Method of Joints exists because EVERY joint in a truss is itself a particle in equilibrium (forces meeting at a point — recall Ch.3!) — so you can march from joint to joint, using ∑Fx=0 and ∑Fy=0 at each one (2 equations, so at most 2 unknown member forces per joint) to solve the entire truss member-by-member.
When to useUse the Method of Joints when you need the force in EVERY (or most) truss member — it systematically solves the whole structure, joint by joint, though it can be tedious for very large trusses.
ExampleA simple triangular truss is built by starting with one triangle (3 members, 3 joints) and successively adding 2 new members plus 1 new joint at a time — this construction rule (from the "simple truss" definition) guarantees the resulting truss is rigid and exactly matches the statically-determinate condition m+r=2j from the next card.
Watch outYou can only start solving a joint once it has AT MOST 2 unknown member forces remaining — jumping to a joint with 3+ unknowns too early leaves you with 2 equations and 3 unknowns (unsolvable at that step); always solve joints in the correct sequence.

Equilibrium in 2-Dimensions (Truss FBD) / Truss Equilibrium Conditions — m+r vs 2j

PurposeGives the determinacy formula: M (members) + R (unknown support reactions) compared against 2J (twice the number of joints, since each joint gives 2 equations) tells you instantly whether a truss is solvable by statics alone (determinate, M+R=2J), unstable (M+R<2J — not enough members to prevent collapse), or indeterminate (M+R>2J — more members than statics alone can solve for).
When to useCheck this formula FIRST, before attempting any truss analysis — it takes seconds to count members, reactions, and joints, and instantly tells you whether the whole problem is even solvable with the tools from this course.
ExampleThe deck's own three cases: (1) M=9, R=3, J=6 → M+R=12=2J=12 → statically Determinate ✓. (2) M=10, R=3, J=6 → M+R=13 > 2J=12 → statically Indeterminate (extra/redundant member present). (3) M=8, R=3, J=6 → M+R=11 < 2J=12 → statically Unstable (the truss would collapse under load, missing a needed member).
Watch outM+R=2J is a NECESSARY condition for determinacy but not always SUFFICIENT — it's possible (though less common) for a truss to satisfy the count exactly and still be improperly constrained (e.g., all reaction forces parallel, unable to resist a load in one direction) — always also sanity-check that the geometry/support arrangement makes physical sense.

Statically Indeterminate Reactions / Joints Under Special Loading / Zero-Force Members

PurposeGives pattern-recognition shortcuts that let you identify SOME member forces by inspection, without any algebra: (1) at a joint with only two non-collinear members and no external load, both members carry ZERO force; (2) at a joint with two collinear members plus a third member, the third member's force equals the external load there (or zero); (3) two collinear members at a joint (with no third member or load) carry EQUAL force.
When to useScan the ENTIRE truss for these zero-force-member patterns BEFORE starting the Method of Joints — eliminating zero-force members first can dramatically simplify the remaining joint equations (fewer unknowns to carry through).
ExamplePer the deck's own truss diagram: members BC, BE, KM, KN, PQ, PN, and LN are all zero-force members, identified purely by inspecting each joint's geometry (unloaded joints with only 2 non-collinear members) — none of these require any equilibrium equation to solve; they're determined instantly by inspection.
Watch outA "zero-force member" carries no force ONLY under the specific loading shown — it's still structurally necessary (often for stability/rigidity, or in case the load changes), so don't mistake "zero force under this load case" for "this member can be removed from the design."

Space Trusses (3D)

PurposeExtends the 2D determinacy logic into 3D: the basic rigid unit is now a tetrahedron (6 members, 4 joints) instead of a triangle, and each joint gives 3 equilibrium equations (not 2) since it's now a 3D particle, giving the 3D determinacy formula m = 3n − 6 (where n = number of joints).
When to useUse m = 3n − 6 (rearranged as m + 6 = 3n, or generally m + r = 3n with r reactions) to check determinacy for any 3D space truss, exactly as you'd use m+r=2j for a 2D truss.
ExampleA simple space truss starts as a tetrahedron: m=6 members, n=4 joints. Check: m = 3n−6 = 3(4)−6 = 6 ✓. Extending it by adding 3 new members and 1 new joint at a time (per the slide's rule) keeps this relationship satisfied at every stage. A real-world analogy: a camera tripod (3 legs meeting at one head) is the simplest possible rigid 3D frame, exactly like a single tetrahedron face — that's why tripods are rigid with no wobble, while a 4-legged table (without a diagonal brace) can rock, because a simple rectangular leg arrangement isn't a triangulated (and therefore not fully rigid) 3D structure.
Watch outThe "6 additional equilibrium equations from the entire truss" mentioned on the slide are NOT independent of the per-joint equations — they're a consequence of them, so don't double-count them as extra information when checking a truss's determinacy.

Method of Sections / Method of Sections (Suitable Cut)

PurposeAn alternative to the Method of Joints for when you only need the force in ONE or a FEW specific members (not the whole truss): "cut" the truss with an imaginary line through the members you want, then apply the 3 rigid-body equilibrium equations (∑Fx=0, ∑Fy=0, ∑M=0) to just ONE resulting half — much faster than solving joint-by-joint from one end all the way to the member you actually care about.
When to useUse the Method of Sections whenever a problem asks for force in just one or two specific "deep inside" members — it skips straight to the answer instead of solving every joint leading up to it.
ExampleTo find force in member BD specifically (not the whole truss), cut through 3 members (n-n) that includes BD, then take the LEFT half as your free body (choosing the side with fewer unknown external loads/reactions, since it's "less desirable" to use the side with more unknowns, per the deck). Apply the 3 equilibrium equations to that half only, treating the three CUT members' forces as unknowns (all initially assumed in tension, per standard convention — the sign of your answer then tells you if it's actually tension or compression).
Watch outThe cutting line must fully separate the truss into two distinct pieces, cutting through AT MOST 3 members if you want to solve directly with the 3 standard equations (3 equations can solve at most 3 unknowns) — a cut through 4+ members leaves the section statically indeterminate using this method alone.

Example 2: Method of Sections (Problem 6.3) — find FH, GH, GI

PurposeA complete worked Method-of-Sections example demonstrating the key efficiency trick: choose your moment point to eliminate 2 of the 3 unknowns at once, so a single moment equation solves directly for the ONE member force you want, without needing simultaneous equations.
When to useWhenever your cut crosses exactly 3 members, take moments about the INTERSECTION POINT of the other two members (the ones you don't currently need) — that single equation isolates your target member force immediately.

Trusses Made of Several Simple Trusses / Trusses & Frames comparison

PurposeExtends the determinacy check to "compound" trusses (multiple simple trusses joined together): m = 2n−3 for a properly rigid compound truss, m = 2n−4 or more for one needing extra reactions, and n = m/2 + r (approximately) as a necessary-but-not-sufficient rigidity condition — this generalizes the simple m+r=2j rule to more complex real bridge/roof geometries.
When to useUse this when a real structure is clearly built from several triangulated sub-trusses connected together (like a large bridge truss made of repeated panel units) rather than one continuous simple-truss construction sequence.
ExampleThe deck contrasts trusses (all members two-force, showing tension/compression labels T/C/C/T/C on its diagram) against frames (at least one multi-force member) — visually, a truss diagram shows every member as a straight line between two pins carrying pure axial force, while a frame diagram shows at least one member with a load applied somewhere along its length (not just at its end pins), requiring both an axial force AND a shear force AND a bending moment to describe it fully.
Watch outA "redundant member" in an indeterminate compound truss isn't wasted — real designs often deliberately add one for extra safety margin (redundancy) — but it does mean statics ALONE can no longer solve for every individual member force; you'd need deformation compatibility equations too (beyond this course).

Analysis of Frame — introduction

PurposeFrames contain at least one multi-force member (3+ forces, not just 2 like a truss member) — this means you CANNOT assume forces act along the member's own axis, so frame analysis needs the full "dismember and FBD each piece separately" approach, unlike the streamlined joint/section methods for pure trusses.
When to useUse frame analysis (not truss methods) whenever ANY member in the structure has a load applied somewhere other than its two end pins, or has 3+ connection points instead of exactly 2.
ExampleA simple A-frame ladder has its two legs meeting at a top pin and a horizontal brace connecting them partway down — the brace is a genuine two-force member (loaded only at its two end pins), but each LEG is a multi-force member if a person's foot load is applied partway up it, requiring the full frame method for the legs even though the brace alone could be treated as a truss member.
Watch outForces between two connected components (at their shared pin) are ALWAYS equal, same line of action, opposite sense (Newton's 3rd law) — a very common frame-analysis mistake is drawing DIFFERENT forces (in magnitude or direction) at the same physical pin on each of the two separate member FBDs.

Analysis of Frame — crane (multi-force member), internal F/V/M

PurposeShows that cutting open a MULTI-force member (unlike a two-force truss member) exposes THREE internal quantities at the cut: axial force F, shear force V, and bending moment M — because a multi-force member resists forces from multiple, non-aligned directions, not just simple tension/compression along its own axis.
When to useUse F, V, M (not just a single axial force) any time you cut open ANY multi-force member (a frame member, or later a beam) to find its internal state at a specific cross-section.
ExampleCutting a crane's vertical support member AD at an interior point J exposes: F (axial force, along the member), V (shear force, perpendicular to the member), and MJ (bending moment at J). By Newton's third law, the internal forces on segment AJ are equal and opposite to those on segment JD — cutting the member always produces a matched force-couple pair at the cut, one on each side.
Watch outA pure two-force truss member never needs V or M (only F, the axial force) — but the moment you have EVEN ONE off-axis load or connection anywhere on a member, you must include shear and bending moment in its internal-force analysis, not just axial force.

Frames That Collapse Without Supports / Example 3: Method of Sections (Problem 6.4) — members ACE and BCD

PurposeSome frames are NOT rigid on their own — remove the external supports and the frame's own pieces can rotate relative to each other, which means the whole-frame FBD alone (3 equations) is not enough to find every internal force; you must ALSO dismember the frame into its individual pieces and write separate equilibrium equations for each piece.
When to useWhenever a frame problem asks for an INTERNAL connection force (like the force at a pin connecting two frame members, or the tension in an internal link), you must dismember the frame — the whole-frame FBD alone cannot reveal internal forces, only external reactions.

PurposeA machine is a non-rigid structure specifically designed to transmit and MODIFY (usually amplify) an input force P into a different output force Q — like a pair of pliers turning a small hand-squeeze into a large gripping force. Because it's non-rigid, you must analyze one of its individual components as your free body, not the whole assembly at once.
When to useUse machine analysis (dismember into individual rigid links, FBD one link at a time) for hand tools, levers, and any mechanism with moving/pivoting parts under load — pliers, bolt cutters, nutcrackers, toggle clamps.
ExampleFor a pair of pliers, summing moments about the pivot A on ONE handle: ∑MA=0 → Pb = Qa (using the deck's own labels, where a and b are the two lever-arm distances from the pivot). Rearranged: Q = Pb/a — if the input force P is applied FARTHER from the pivot than the output force Q's contact point (b > a), the tool mechanically amplifies force (Q > P), which is exactly how pliers and bolt cutters achieve high gripping/cutting force from a modest hand squeeze.
Watch outA machine's mechanical advantage depends entirely on the RATIO of lever arms (b/a) — students sometimes assume all hand tools automatically amplify force, but a mechanism can just as easily REDUCE force while increasing motion/reach instead (a tradeoff, not a free amplification, since energy/work is conserved in the idealized frictionless case).

Internal Forces (Beams)

PurposeA beam bends internally under transverse (perpendicular) loads, producing internal SHEAR (V) and BENDING MOMENT (M) at every cross-section along its length — engineers need to know how V and M vary along the beam to design it (thicker/stronger where V and M are largest).
When to useTo find V and M at a specific point C: (1) find the beam's support reactions using the WHOLE beam as a free body, (2) cut the beam at C, (3) use EITHER resulting piece's free body (whichever has fewer forces) to solve for V and M at that cut via equilibrium.
ExampleA simply-supported beam (pin at one end, roller at the other) carries a single concentrated load at its midpoint. Cutting the beam just to the LEFT of the load and using the left piece (which only has the left support's reaction acting on it): ∑Fy=0 on that piece gives V directly (equal to the support reaction, since nothing else acts on that short segment), and ∑M=0 about the cut gives M directly.
Watch outAlways find the SUPPORT REACTIONS from the WHOLE beam FIRST — trying to find V and M at an internal point without first knowing the reactions is like trying to solve an equation with an extra unknown still hiding in it.

Definition of Stress

PurposeStress (Force ÷ Area) exists because the SAME force can be safe or catastrophic depending on how much area it's spread over — a knife cuts because it concentrates force onto a tiny edge area, creating enormous stress, while the same force applied with a flat palm is harmless. Stress is what actually determines whether a material fails, not force alone.
When to useCompute stress (not just force) any time you're comparing a load against a material's strength limit — material failure criteria (like "this steel yields at 250 MPa") are always stated in terms of stress, never raw force, because the SAME steel bar can safely carry very different forces depending on its cross-sectional area.
ExampleUsing the deck's own worked numbers: a bar carries tension T = 1000 lb (1 kip) over cross-sectional area A = 10 in². Stress = Force/Area = 1000/10 = 100 lb/in² (psi). Converting to SI: T = 1000 lb × 4.448 N/lb = 4448 N; A = 10 in² × (25.4 mm)² = 6450 mm². Stress = 4448/6450 ≈ 0.69 N/mm² = 0.69 MPa (matching the deck's stated result).
Watch outNever compare a raw FORCE directly against a material strength rating — always convert to stress (divide by the actual cross-sectional area) first; comparing forces alone ignores how concentrated or spread-out that force actually is.

Definition of Strain

PurposeStrain (ΔL / L₀, change in length divided by original length) is the dimensionless measure of how much a material stretches RELATIVE to its own original size — this normalization is what lets engineers compare deformation across objects of totally different sizes on a fair, apples-to-apples basis.
When to useUse strain (not raw elongation ΔL) whenever comparing how "stretchy" different materials or different-sized specimens are — a 1 mm stretch means something very different for a 10 mm wire versus a 10 m cable, but the STRAIN normalizes that difference away.
ExampleUsing the deck's own numbers: a bar with original length L₀ = 10 in. stretches by ΔL = 0.12 in. under load. Strain = ΔL/L₀ = 0.12/10 = 0.012 in./in. Since both numerator and denominator are lengths, their units cancel — strain is a pure (dimensionless) number, and critically, it's the SAME numeric value whether measured in inches, meters, or any other consistent length unit.
Watch outStudents sometimes try to assign strain a "unit" like "in/in" as if it were a real physical unit needing conversion — strain is dimensionless (the units genuinely cancel), so a strain value never changes when you switch from SI to English units, unlike stress or force.

Relations Among Load, Shear, and Bending Moment

PurposeEstablishes the calculus relationships between a beam's distributed load w(x), its internal shear V(x), and its internal bending moment M(x): the slope of the shear diagram equals −w(x), and the slope of the moment diagram equals V(x) — letting engineers sketch V and M diagrams directly from the load diagram without re-cutting the beam at every single point.
When to useUse these slope relationships to sketch (not just calculate point-by-point) the full shear and moment diagrams along an entire beam — essential for finding the MAXIMUM moment location (which occurs where V = 0, i.e. where the moment diagram's slope is zero).
ExampleWhere a beam has NO distributed load (w=0), the shear diagram is FLAT (constant V, since dV/dx = −w = 0) and the moment diagram is a straight sloped line (constant slope = V). Where a beam has a UNIFORM distributed load (w=constant), the shear diagram is a straight sloped line and the moment diagram is a curved parabola. A concentrated point load causes a sudden JUMP (discontinuity) in the shear diagram at that point, equal in size to the point load.
Watch outThe maximum bending moment (usually the critical value for design) occurs where the SHEAR diagram crosses zero — students sometimes look for the maximum moment at the location of the largest applied load instead, which is often wrong; you must actually track where V changes sign.

Introduction (Friction)

PurposeEstablishes that NO real surface is perfectly frictionless — friction is the tangential (surface-parallel) resistance force that develops whenever you try to slide one surface relative to another in contact, and it exists because of microscopic surface roughness/adhesion, not because of some separate "friction force generator."
When to useInclude a friction force term in your FBD any time two real surfaces are in contact and there's a tendency (or actual) relative sliding — unless the problem explicitly states the surface is "smooth" or "frictionless," don't assume friction is absent.
ExampleA book resting motionless on a slightly tilted table: even though nothing is visibly moving, static friction is actively pushing UP-the-slope on the book, exactly balancing the down-slope component of gravity — this invisible balancing act is friction "at work" even in a completely static (unmoving) situation.
Watch outFriction forces are LIMITED in magnitude (up to some maximum value) — they don't grow without bound to prevent all motion; once the applied force exceeds that maximum, sliding begins regardless of how much friction "wants" to resist it.
pg 170

Friction Force — the four properties

PurposeSummarizes friction's four defining characteristics: it results from contact between two bodies, it always opposes (slows down) relative motion or the tendency toward motion, and it's proportional to the NORMAL force pressing the two surfaces together (not to the contact area, a common misconception).
When to useUse "friction ∝ Normal force" (Ff = μFN) whenever estimating how much friction resistance to expect — and remember this means a heavier object (bigger FN) has proportionally more friction resisting it, even with the identical contact material.
ExampleTwo identical wooden blocks, one twice as heavy as the other, both resting on the same wood surface (μs = 0.4, from the table on the next card): the heavier block's maximum static friction is proportionally twice as large as the lighter block's, because Ff,max = μs × FN, and FN doubles when weight doubles — but the ratio Ff,max/FN (i.e., μs) stays exactly the same for both blocks, since it's a property of the two materials in contact, not of the specific weight.
Watch outFriction depends on the NORMAL force, NOT the contact area — a common misconception is that a wider tire or a bigger block "grips more" because of more surface area; physically (in the simple Coulomb friction model used in this course), friction force is independent of contact area entirely.

The Laws of Dry Friction — static vs kinetic transition

PurposeDescribes the actual PHYSICAL SEQUENCE as force P is gradually increased on a stationary block: static friction F grows to match P exactly (keeping the block at rest) UP TO a maximum value Fm; beyond Fm, the block starts moving and friction DROPS to a smaller kinetic-friction value Fk — this explains the everyday experience that it takes more force to START moving something than to KEEP it moving.
When to useUse the FOUR distinct regimes (No friction when Px=0; No motion when Px<Fm; Motion impending when Px=Fm exactly; Motion when Px>Fm) to correctly identify which friction formula applies to a given problem — "impending motion" problems specifically use Ff = μsFN (the MAXIMUM static value), while "already sliding" problems use Ff = μkFN.
ExamplePushing a heavy couch across a floor: it takes a large initial push to get it moving (overcoming maximum static friction, μs), but once it's sliding, a noticeably SMALLER continuous push keeps it moving at constant speed (kinetic friction, μk < μs) — this everyday experience is the direct real-world confirmation of Fk < Fs.
Watch out"Motion impending" (Px = Fm exactly) is a very common problem phrasing that specifically signals: use μs (not μk) and set friction at its MAXIMUM value, since the object is right on the verge of sliding but hasn't started yet.

Types of Friction / Normal Force / Static & Kinetic Friction Coefficients (table)

PurposeGives the formula Ff = μFN and a reference table of typical μs, μk values for common material pairs — real designers look up (or measure) these coefficients since friction depends entirely on WHICH two materials are in contact, not on any universal constant.
When to useAlways find the Normal force FIRST (usually from ∑Fy=0, since FN is "usually found by summing forces in the y-direction" per the slide), THEN multiply by the appropriate μ to get the friction force.
ExampleUsing the deck's own table: steel on steel has μs = 0.74, μk = 0.57. Once sliding, the resisting friction drops to Ff = μk × FN = 0.57 × 490.5 ≈ 280 N.

Static Vs. Dynamic Friction — summary

PurposeConsolidates the key comparison: kinetic friction is always weaker than static friction (Fk < Fs) and, importantly, the coefficient of KINETIC friction does NOT change with the object's speed once it's sliding — a simplification that makes kinetic friction problems solvable with one constant μk value.
When to useUse this "speed-independence" assumption to justify treating μk as a single constant number throughout a sliding-friction problem, even if the object's speed changes during the motion.
ExampleA hockey puck sliding across ice experiences kinetic friction that (in this idealized Coulomb-friction model) is the SAME whether the puck is moving fast (right after a hit) or slowing down near the end of its slide — the friction force stays Ff = μk·FN throughout, not increasing or decreasing with the puck's changing speed.
Watch outThis speed-independence is a simplifying MODEL assumption (Coulomb/dry friction theory), not a universal physical law — real friction can vary somewhat with speed in some regimes, but this course consistently uses the constant-μk idealization for all problems.

Friction Example (Problem 8.2-style) — crate on an incline

PurposeThe deck's closing example is a complete "does it actually slide?" friction problem — the single most important friction skill: first assume equilibrium and find the friction force REQUIRED to hold the object still, then compare that against the MAXIMUM available static friction to determine whether the object actually stays put or breaks loose, and if it slides, find the resulting kinetic friction force.
When to useUse this exact three-step sequence (required force → compare to maximum → kinetic force if it slides) any time a problem asks whether an object "remains in equilibrium," "stays at rest," or "starts to move" under a combination of applied and gravitational forces with friction present.
ExampleA 300 N crate rests on an incline (3-4-5 triangle, so the incline makes an angle with cos = 4/5, sin = 3/5) with a 100 N applied force up the slope; μs = 0.25, μk = 0.20. Step 1 — force required for equilibrium: ∑Fx=0: 100 N − (3/5)(300 N) − F = 0 → F = −80 N, so F = 80 N directed up the incline is required to hold the crate still. ∑Fy=0: N − (4/5)(300 N) = 0 → N = 240 N. Step 2 — compare to maximum available friction: Fm = μsN = 0.25 × 240 = 60 N. Since the 80 N required exceeds the 60 N available, equilibrium is NOT possible — the crate slides down the incline. Step 3 — actual kinetic friction once sliding: Factual = μkN = 0.20 × 240 = 48 N, acting up the incline (opposing the downward sliding motion). The net unbalanced force on the crate is then (3/5)(300) − 100 − 48 = 32 N down the incline, confirming it accelerates down the slope.
Watch outNotice which coefficient is used at each step: the COMPARISON step (is 80 N > 60 N?) uses μs, since you're testing whether static friction CAN hold the object — but once you've established the object is actually sliding, the friction force it actually experiences switches to the smaller μk value. Using μs throughout (forgetting to switch to μk after concluding the object slides) is the most common mistake on this exact problem type.
