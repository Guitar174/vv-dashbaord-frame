Splitting "Mechanics" into smaller boxes

The idea — why we sort things into boxes at all Mechanics is a huge subject — way too big to study all at once. So before doing any math, scientists first sort every possible situation into smaller, clearly labeled boxes, the same way a doctor first asks "is this a heart problem, a bone problem, or a skin problem?" before deciding how to treat you. There are 3 splits, one inside the other, like Russian nesting dolls. Let's go through them one at a time, slowly.
Split 1 of 3 — Solid vs. Fluid Look at any object. Does it hold its own shape when nothing is touching it — like a rock, a chair, a phone? That's solid mechanics. Does it flow and just take the shape of whatever container it's poured into — like water, air, honey? That's fluid mechanics.

This course only deals with solids. We will never study liquids or gases in this class — that's a completely different course.

3 more examples to make the split click:
  1. A steel nail keeps its shape no matter what — solid. The gasoline sitting inside a car's tank takes the shape of the tank — fluid.
  2. A basketball's rubber shell holds its round shape — solid. The air pumped inside it spreads out to fill the whole space — fluid.
  3. A wooden table stays a table — solid. Milk poured into a glass takes the glass's shape — fluid.
Split 2 of 3 — Rigid vs. Deformable (this split only happens INSIDE "solid") Now zoom in further, just on solids. Some solid objects bend or stretch pretty noticeably when you push on them — a rubber band, a diving board, a fishing rod. We call these deformable bodies. Other solid objects seem to stay exactly the same shape no matter how hard you push — a steel beam, a brick, a bolt. We call these rigid bodies.

Here's an honest confession: nothing is EVER perfectly rigid in real life. Even a thick steel beam bends a microscopic amount when you lean on it — you just can't see it with your eyes. But that tiny bit of bending is usually so small it doesn't change our answer in any way that matters. So "rigid body" isn't really a claim about how the universe works — it's a simplifying promise we choose to make: "for this problem, I'll pretend this object can't bend at all, because the real bending is too tiny to care about." Every single problem in this entire course accepts that promise.
Split 3 of 3 — Statics vs. Dynamics (this split only happens INSIDE "rigid") Finally, zoom in one more time, just on rigid bodies. Some rigid objects are just sitting there, not going anywhere — a parked car, a bridge, a book on a table. Others are actually moving in a CHANGING way — a car speeding up at a green light, a ball you just threw, a rocket launching.
  • Statics = the object is either completely still, OR it's moving in a straight line at one constant speed that never changes (like a boat drifting at a perfectly steady speed). Either way, nothing about its motion is changing.
  • Dynamics = the object IS speeding up, slowing down, turning, or otherwise changing how it's moving.
Putting all 3 splits together — the one sentence to remember Here's the full nested path, written out as one line: Mechanics → Solid (not fluid) → Rigid (not deformable) → Statics (not dynamics). This whole class lives inside that smallest, most specific box. That means every single object we study this semester is: (1) something solid you could physically hold, (2) something we're allowed to pretend never bends at all, and (3) something that is either completely still, or moving at one unchanging speed.

If you ever get lost mid-problem, come back to this one sentence: "This object isn't going anywhere, and it isn't bending — I just need to figure out what forces are keeping it that way." That sentence is true for every problem, in every chapter, all semester long.

Above — Statics, Dynamics, and a NEW third name: "Mechanics of Materials" This slide re-states the Statics/Dynamics split from the last page (statics = at rest or moving at a constant, unchanging speed; dynamics = allowed to accelerate) — but then it introduces one more important idea: inside "static equilibrium," there are actually two different branches of study, which ask two very different questions about the SAME still object:
  • Statics (this course) asks: "is the WHOLE object balanced?" It treats the object as one solid rigid chunk and only uses the equilibrium equations (ΣF=0, ΣM=0) to find outside/support forces. It never asks what's happening INSIDE the material.

Step 2 (Mechanics of Materials) takes that force and checks whether the rope's actual material (its thickness, what it's made of) can handle that much pulling without snapping. A true, scary story: the day a hotel walkway collapsed

The idea — let's slow down and really understand this story This is the best example of why Statics matters, so let's go through it carefully, like a story.

On July 17, 1981, in a hotel called the Hyatt Regency in Kansas City, there was a beautiful lobby with three walkways (like indoor balconies/bridges) hanging above it, one on top of another, held up by long metal rods from the ceiling — kind of like three shelves hanging from strings.

The original plan (the safe one): Each walkway was supposed to have its own separate rod running all the way from the ceiling down to it. Picture three different ropes, each one holding up its own single bucket. Each rope only has to hold the weight of ONE bucket.

What actually got built (the dangerous change): During construction, someone changed the design. Instead of each walkway having its own separate rod, they made the upper walkway's rod continue down and ALSO hold up the lower walkway. Picture ONE rope holding up two buckets stacked on top of each other, connected by that same rope. Now, look closely at where the top bucket connects to the rope — that connection point has to hold the weight of BOTH buckets, not just one!

This small-looking change doubled the force pulling on that one connection point on the upper walkway. Nobody re-checked whether that connection was strong enough for double the force. On the day of the accident, that connection point finally gave way under all that extra pulling force. The upper walkway crashed down onto the lower one, and both crashed down onto the crowded lobby below.
Why this story matters for YOU, right now 114 people died, and 216 more were hurt — making it (at the time) the deadliest building collapse in U.S. history. The engineers who approved that small-looking construction change lost their engineering licenses, and the courts made them pay over $140 million to victims and families.
The one lesson to remember forever A "small" change to how forces travel through a structure can secretly double (or worse) the force at the weakest point — and nobody notices unless someone actually sits down and calculates it. That calculating is exactly what this entire Statics course teaches you how to do. It's not just abstract math for a test — it's literally the skill that could stop a building from falling down.

Above — the 4 basic ingredients, with ALL of their properties Every single problem in this class, no matter how complicated it looks, is built from just 4 basic ingredients. Think of these like the 4 basic ingredients of cooking (heat, food, water, time) — everything else is just a combination of these. Let's list every property the slide actually gives for each one:
  • Length — (1) locates the position of a point in space, and (2) describes the size of a physical system. Example: "the beam is 3 meters long" uses length both ways — 3 meters is its SIZE, and where it starts/ends locates its POSITION.
  • Time — a succession of events (one thing happening after another). Important: statics problems are time-independent — meaning time doesn't even appear in our equations this semester, because nothing is changing from one moment to the next.
  • Mass — a property of matter that gives a quantitative measure of an object's resistance to a change in velocity. In plain words: mass measures how "stubborn" an object is about speeding up or slowing down (this stubbornness is called inertia). A bowling ball resists being sped up much more than a balloon of the same size, because it has more mass.
  • Force — a push or pull exerted by one body on another, characterized by 4 things: magnitude (how strong, with a unit like Newtons), direction (which way), sense (which of the two ways ALONG that direction — e.g. "along this line, but pushing vs. pulling"), and point of application (exactly where on the object it touches).
Below — the SAME 4 ingredients again, but described more precisely. Let's go bullet by bullet, slowly. This slide is titled the same as slide 1 ("Basic Quantities Used in Mechanics"), but it gives sharper, more careful definitions of the same 4 ingredients. Don't skim past it — each bullet adds something slide 1 didn't fully spell out.

Bullet 1 — "Space - associated with the notion of the position of a point P given in terms of three coordinates measured from a reference point or origin." This is really just a more careful version of "Length." To say WHERE a point is in space, we need 3 numbers (called coordinates) — one for each direction (like left-right, up-down, forward-back). But those 3 numbers only mean something once you pick a starting point to measure FROM — that starting point is called the origin. Without an origin, "5 meters" is meaningless — 5 meters from WHERE? Think of GPS coordinates: your phone's location is 2 numbers (latitude, longitude) measured from a fixed reference point on Earth. Space in mechanics works the same way, just with 3 numbers instead of 2, and we'll build this idea properly with x, y, z axes on page 31.

Bullet 2 — "Time - conceived as a succession of events. Definition of an event requires specification of the time AND position at which it occurred. Concepts of STATICS are time-independent." Here's the careful part: to fully describe something happening (an "event"), you need to say BOTH when it happened AND where it happened — just saying "3 o'clock" isn't a complete event; you also need to say WHERE at 3 o'clock. This matters a lot in the Dynamics course (where objects move to different positions at different times). But this slide makes a really important promise specifically for OUR course: "Concepts of STATICS are time-independent." That means in every single problem we solve this semester, time simply never appears in our equations — because nothing is changing from one moment to the next, there's no "when" to track. This is one of the biggest reasons Statics is easier than Dynamics.

Bullet 3 — "Mass – used to characterize bodies. A property of matter by which we can compare the action of one body to another. This property provides a qualitative measure that does not change from one location to another." Let's unpack "compare the action of one body to another": imagine pushing two different objects with the exact same force — mass is the property that tells you WHY they respond differently. The heavier one (more mass) barely moves; the lighter one (less mass) shoots off quickly. So mass isn't just "how much stuff" in an abstract sense — it's specifically the property that lets us COMPARE and PREDICT how two different bodies will react to the same push. And crucially: this measure "does not change from one location to another" — take that same object to the Moon, to space, anywhere — its mass stays exactly the same number.

Bullet 4 — "Force – represents the action of one body on another. A force is characterized by its point of application, magnitude (unit), and direction, i.e., a force is a vector quantity. Interaction occurs when there is direct contact between the bodies. Gravitational, electrical, and magnetic forces do not require direct contact." This bullet does two important jobs. First, it officially tells us a force is a vector quantity — meaning (as we'll properly learn in Chapter 2) it has both a size AND a direction, and needs special math rules to add up correctly, unlike plain numbers. Second, it draws a genuinely important line: MOST forces need direct contact between the two bodies to happen at all (like your hand pushing a door — they have to be touching). But gravitational, electrical, and magnetic forces are the exception — they can reach across empty space and act on an object with nothing touching it at all.

Above — Length, Time, and Mass are independent... but Force is NOT In Newtonian Mechanics, length, time, and mass don't depend on each other at all — you can change one without automatically changing another. But Force is different: it depends on both the mass of the body AND how quickly its velocity is changing over time. This slide also gives the OFFICIAL definitions of our two idealizations from before: Particle = an object that has mass, but whose size is neglected — the geometry (shape) of the body is not involved in the analysis at all. Rigid Body = a combination of a huge number of particles that stay in a FIXED position relative to each other, both before AND after a force is applied (i.e., it truly cannot change shape).
Below — a THIRD idealization we haven't met yet: "Concentrated Force" Alongside Particle and Rigid Body, there's a third simplifying trick: a Concentrated Force is when we assume a force acts at a single exact POINT on a body, even though in real life every force is actually spread out over some small area of contact. This is justified whenever that contact area is small compared to the overall size of the body — like a train wheel resting on a rail: the contact patch is tiny compared to the whole wheel, so we just draw the force as one arrow at one point.

Above — Sir Isaac Newton (1642–1729) and his book, "Principia" In the year 1687, Isaac Newton published a book called Principia in which he formulated three laws — and the slide states plainly that ALL conventional motion is based on these three laws. That's not an exaggeration: literally everything in this entire course (and the follow-up Dynamics course) rests on just these three rules Newton wrote down over 300 years ago.
Below — Newton's First Law, stated precisely The slide calls this the "basis of rigid body mechanics" — meaning everything else in the course builds on it. Here's the official statement: "If the resultant force on a particle is zero, the particle will remain at rest or continue to move in a straight line." In everyday words: an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at a constant velocity, UNLESS an unbalanced force acts on it.

Written as a formula: if ΣF = 0, then v = constant ("v" stands for velocity). The slide also introduces the exact term we'll use constantly: State of Equilibrium — equilibrium exists when all the forces on a particle are "in balance." When a particle is in equilibrium, its velocity does not change at all.
pg 19

Two slides here: unpacking the First Law fully, then the Second Law

Above — "Interpretation of First Law" — 5 things this slide wants you to really understand
  1. In other words: objects keep doing what they're already doing, unless a force acts on them.
  2. A body is in equilibrium if it moves with a constant velocity. Being completely at rest is just a SPECIAL CASE of constant velocity, where that constant happens to be zero (v = 0 = constant).
  3. An object at rest AND an object moving at constant velocity both have zero acceleration — neither one is speeding up or slowing down.
  4. For a body to be in equilibrium, the resultant (net) force — meaning the full vector addition of every force acting on it — must be zero.
  5. Net force = the sum of ALL forces acting on the body, added together as vectors (we'll learn exactly how to add forces like this in Chapter 2).
Below — Newton's Second Law: the "how hard do I have to push" rule This law only matters when something is NOT in equilibrium — meaning it's actually speeding up or slowing down. Official statement: "A particle acted upon by an unbalanced force F experiences an acceleration that has the same direction as the force, and a magnitude that is directly proportional to the force." The formula is the most famous equation in all of physics:

F = m × a
  • F = the force (the push or pull)
  • m = the mass (how much "stuff" the object is made of)
  • a = the acceleration (how quickly its speed is changing)
The slide highlights two specific relationships: acceleration is directly proportional to the force (push 3 times harder → 3 times the acceleration), and acceleration is inversely proportional to the mass (if one object has 5 times more mass than another, and you push both equally, the heavier one accelerates at only 1/5th the rate).

Good news for this whole course: since Statics only deals with things that are NOT accelerating, "a" is always zero for us. This Second Law mostly stays in the background this semester — but it's the reason the First Law (equilibrium) works the way it does.

Two slides here: Newton's Third Law stated formally, then a hands-on example

Above — Newton's Third Law (the official statement) The official statement: "The forces of action and reaction between two particles have the same magnitude and line of action (collinear), with opposite sense. For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction." Written as a formula: FAB = −FBA. In plain words: Newton's Third Law says "whenever one thing pushes on another thing, the second thing pushes back just as hard, in the opposite direction." Suppose body A exerts a force on body B — experiments show that body B always exerts an equal and oppositely-directed force back on body A. The slide flags something important in red: these two forces act on DIFFERENT objects, so they do NOT cancel each other out.
Below — Example of Newton's 3rd Law (pressing on a desk) Try this right now: press your hand flat on a desk. Your hand exerts a force ON the desk, and the desk exerts a force back ON your hand. These are exactly the "action" and "reaction" forces the slide names. They will always have the same magnitude and act along the same line, but point in opposite senses (directions). If the desk didn't push back, your hand would simply sink through it.

Above — Newton's Law of Gravitation, done properly with the actual formula A fun historical correction first: Newton did NOT "discover" gravity (people already knew things fell down!). What he actually did was propose that gravity is universal — that literally ALL objects in the universe attract every other object, not just "big things pulling on us."

Two particles are attracted to each other with equal and opposite forces. That attraction is directly dependent on the masses of both objects (bigger masses = stronger pull) and inversely proportional to the SQUARE of the distance between their centers (double the distance = only 1/4 the pull, not 1/2 — this is called an "inverse square law"). The actual formula is:

F = G·M·m / r²
  • M and m = the masses of the two particles
  • G = the universal gravitational constant (a fixed number: 6.673 × 10⁻¹¹ m³/kg·s²) — its precise value was measured experimentally by a scientist named Henry Cavendish, in the century AFTER Newton had already died
  • r = the distance between the two particles' centers
  • F comes out in Newtons
The slide also reminds us of two formulas we'll use constantly: F = mg (weight, a special simplified case of gravity just for objects near Earth's surface) and g = 9.81 m/s² (Earth's gravitational acceleration).
Below — System of Units/Dimensions: SI vs. FPS, and which quantity is "basic" in each A "unit" is just the name we give to a physical quantity, so everyone measures the same way. There are two systems used in engineering:
  1. International System of Units (SI) — the basic quantities are length, time, and mass, measured in meter (m), second (s), and kilogram (kg). Force is a DERIVED quantity (built from the other three), measured in a unit called the Newton, using F = ma.
  2. U.S. Customary Units (FPS) — the basic quantities are length, time, and FORCE, measured in foot (ft), second (s), and pound (lb). Here, mass is the one that's derived instead.
This is the single most important difference between the two systems: SI treats mass as fundamental and calculates force from it; FPS treats force as fundamental and calculates mass from it.

Above — Rules For Use of Units & Prefixes
  • Unit names are always written in LOWERCASE letters, except for M, G, T, and units named after a person (like Newton, N — capitalized because it honors Isaac Newton).
  • When a quantity is defined by several units multiplied together, separate them with a dot: "m·s" means meter-seconds, which is completely different from "ms," which means milliseconds! (These slides use a dash instead of a dot for this, but the idea is the same — don't let "m-s" and "ms" look interchangeable.)
  • When performing calculations, begin by writing every number out in terms of BASE units. Worked example from the slide: (50 kN)(60 nm) = (50)(10³)(N) × (60)(10⁻⁹)(m) = 3000×10⁻⁶ N·m = 3 N·mm.
  • Compound prefixes should NOT be used — for example, never write "kμs" (kilo-micro-seconds); just convert to a single proper prefix instead.
  • Avoid using prefixes in the DENOMINATOR (bottom) of a combined unit, except for kg (kilogram is special because it's the SI base unit for mass, even though "kilo" is technically a prefix).
Below — Let's actually DO a unit conversion together, step by step Here's a secret that makes unit conversion easy: you're allowed to multiply anything by 1 without changing its actual value. Since "1 inch = 25.4 mm" is a true fact, that means the fraction (25.4 mm ÷ 1 inch) is secretly just another way of writing the number "1" — it's a fraction that equals exactly one, just written with different units on the top and bottom. Multiplying by it doesn't change the real amount, only how we write it — kind of like how "1 dollar" and "100 cents" are the exact same amount of money, just written differently. The slide also notes: whichever unit you WANT goes on top of your conversion fraction, and whichever unit you're trying to CANCEL goes on the bottom.
The problem Convert a torque (twisting force) measurement of 47 in·lb (inch-pounds, an American unit) into SI units (Newton-meters).
How to solve it — nice and slow We know: 1 inch = 25.4 mm, and 1 pound (lb) = 4.448 Newtons (N).

Step 1 — swap out the "inch" part: We multiply our 47 in·lb by the fraction (25.4 mm / 1 in). The word "in" (inch) appears on the top of our starting number and on the bottom of our fraction, so they cancel each other out — just like how in the fraction (2×3)/2, the 2's cancel.
47 in·lb × (25.4 mm / 1 in) = 1193.8 mm·lb

Step 2 — swap out the "lb" (pound) part: Now we multiply by (4.448 N / 1 lb). The "lb" cancels out the same way.
1193.8 mm·lb × (4.448 N / 1 lb) = 5310.0 mm·N

Step 3 — turn millimeters into meters: Since 1000 mm = 1 m, we multiply by (1 m / 1000 mm).
5310.0 mm·N × (1 m / 1000 mm) = 5.31 N·m
Let's double-check our own work (always a good habit!) 47 × 25.4 = 1193.8. Then 1193.8 × 4.448 = 5310.0. Then divide by 1000 to get 5.310. So our final answer, 5.31 N·m, matches. Common beginner mistake: converting only ONE of the two units (only the inches, or only the pounds) and forgetting the other one — always check that you've swapped out every single unit before you're done.

  • Choose a convenient axis for calculating the net torque on the object. The choice of axis is arbitrary — you're allowed to pick whichever point makes the math easiest.

Above — Dimensional Homogeneity Here's a simple but powerful rule called dimensional homogeneity: every single term you're adding together in an equation MUST have the same units. Two slides here: What is a Force?, and Force System Terminology

Above — What is A Force? A force is an effect that tends to change the state of rest or uniform motion of a body. It cannot be seen — only the EFFECT of a force on a body may be seen. Force units: Newton (N) or kilonewton (kN) in SI. A force is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction. Concurrent: all forces act through (pass through) the same single point.
Below — Force System Terminology Line of Action of a force is the straight line that's collinear with (lies along) the force vector. Collinear: a system of forces that all share the same line of action. Two forces are equivalent if they have the same effect on a rigid body — meaning the forces must be equal AND have the same moment about any point. A push on a body is called a compressive force; a pull is called a tensile force. According to the principle of transmissibility, all forces are "sliding vectors" — they're equivalent forces even though their point of application can be transmitted (slid) anywhere along their own line of action.

Two slides here: External Forces on a Rigid Body, and Internal Forces on a Rigid Body

Above — External Forces on Rigid Body These forces are external to the rigid body and represent the action of OTHER bodies on it. Two sub-types worth knowing: Support Reactions — the forces responsible for keeping a rigid body at rest, caused by the loads applied to it (a consequence of equilibrium).   7. Unknown forces are represented by letters and arrows (an assumed direction).

Above — Vector Addition (Parallelogram Law) Imagine a book given a 2nd displacement V (after the first, U). The book's FINAL position is the same whether you give it displacement U then V, or V then U — U and V together are equivalent to one single displacement W: U + V = W. In the Parallelogram Law, the two vectors are always drawn concurrent at the Tail (starting from the same point). A parallelogram is a 4-sided figure with opposite sides parallel; the diagonal is the straight line joining two opposite corners — and that diagonal IS the resultant W.
Below — Vector Addition (Triangle Law) begins The Parallelogram Law says the addition of two vectors A and B gives a resultant vector R. That same resultant R can ALSO be found by Triangle construction (connecting them Tip-to-Tail instead). Vector addition is Commutative: R = A + B = B + A — you get the same answer regardless of which vector you draw first.

Breaking one force into two "component" forces (a game-changing idea)

The idea So far we've been combining several forces INTO one. Now we do the opposite: taking ONE force and splitting it into two easier pieces. It's usually most useful to split a force into a horizontal piece and a vertical piece, because those two pieces don't interfere with each other at all — you can deal with "sideways" and "up-down" completely separately, which makes the math MUCH simpler.

We write this as: F = Fx + Fy, where Fx = the horizontal piece, and Fy = the vertical piece. Using the i and j unit vectors: F = Fxi + Fyj.
pg 52

The exact formulas for splitting a force, and re-combining several forces

The idea — splitting formulas If a force F points at angle θ above the horizontal:
  • Fx = F·cos(θ) (the horizontal piece)
  • Fy = F·sin(θ) (the vertical piece)
And to go backwards (find the angle if you only know the pieces): θ = tan⁻¹(Fy/Fx).
The idea — why this makes adding forces so much easier Since x-pieces and y-pieces never mix, we can add up ALL the x-pieces of every force separately (ordinary addition, no angles needed!), and separately add up ALL the y-pieces. This turns a hard geometry problem into simple arithmetic.
pg 53

The general recipe for adding any number of forces

The idea For ANY number of forces P, Q, S, and so on, the resultant's pieces are found by simple addition:

Rx = ΣFx (add up every force's x-piece)   and   Ry = ΣFy (add up every force's y-piece)

Then, to turn those two pieces back into one single resultant force: R = √(Rx² + Ry²) (this is just the Pythagorean theorem!), and its angle is θ = tan⁻¹(Ry/Rx).

Part 4 — When Forces Balance Perfectly (Equilibrium)

pages 56–62
pg 56

Equilibrium of a particle, and "net force" vocabulary

The idea — equilibrium Remember from Chapter 1: when ALL the forces on an object add up to exactly zero, the object is in equilibrium — it stays still, or keeps moving at a perfectly steady speed. With just 2 forces, equilibrium means they must be exactly equal size, same line, but opposite direction (like a tug-of-war that's perfectly tied). With 3 or more forces, if you draw them tip-to-tail (Triangle Law, extended), a body in equilibrium will form a closed shape (a polygon) that comes right back to where it started — no gap left over.
The idea — net force vocabulary
  • Net force (also called "resultant force") = what's left over after adding up ALL the forces on an object.
  • Balanced forces — forces that cancel out completely (net force = zero).
  • Unbalanced forces — forces that do NOT fully cancel; they cause the object to speed up, slow down, or change direction (this is Chapter 1's Second Law territory, not this course's main focus).
pg 57

Drawing an FBD for a hanging weight, and understanding "Tension"

The idea A Space Diagram is just a normal sketch of the real-life setup (like two poles with ropes holding up a heavy truck-like weight in between). The Free Body Diagram then isolates just the single connection point, showing only the forces pulling on it (the rope tensions and the weight).

Ropes and cables can ONLY pull — never push. This is a super important physical fact: you can pull a dog on a leash, but you can't PUSH a dog by shoving on a floppy leash — it would just go limp. This means every rope/cable force in an FBD must point AWAY from the object, along the rope, toward wherever the rope is anchored. This kind of pulling force is called Tension.
pg 58

Compression, and how a pulley works

The idea — compression Unlike a rope, a solid rigid bar (like a wooden beam) CAN push as well as pull — this pushing is called Compression. Think of stacking bricks: each brick below is being squeezed (compressed) by the weight of all the bricks above it.
The idea — pulleys A pulley is just a wheel that a rope wraps around — its whole job is to CHANGE THE DIRECTION of a pulling force, without changing its size. If you pull down on a rope with 50 N of force, and that rope goes over a pulley and connects to a bucket, the bucket feels 50 N of upward pull too — same size, just redirected. This is how old wells worked: pulling down was easier than lifting straight up, even though the physical amount of force needed was the same.

1D and 2D Free Body Diagrams, side by side

The idea A 1D (one-dimensional) FBD only has forces along a single straight line — like a gymnast hanging straight down from a rope: just tension pulling up, and weight pulling down, both along the same vertical line.

A 2D (two-dimensional) FBD has forces spread across a flat plane — like a car resting on a sloped ramp: gravity pulls straight down, but the ramp's push (normal force) is perpendicular to the RAMP's surface, not straight up — so we have to break the weight into components (using the tilt angle) to line everything up properly with the ramp's own tilted x-y axes.

Adding a third direction: z (depth)

The idea Everything we did with x and y (2D) extends naturally into 3D by adding a third direction, z. A vector A in 3D is written: A = Axi + Ayj + Azk. Its total length (magnitude) is found with a 3D version of the Pythagorean theorem: A = √(Ax² + Ay² + Az²). It's the exact same idea as 2D — just one more number to keep track of.

Adding 3D vectors, and 3D position vectors

The idea — adding 3D vectors Exactly like 2D: add matching pieces together. If A = Axi+Ayj+Azk and B = Bxi+Byj+Bzk, then A+B = (Ax+Bx)i + (Ay+By)j + (Az+Bz)k.

pg 65

Describing a 3D force two different ways

The idea — Method 1: using given angles Sometimes a problem tells you the angle a force makes with one axis (say, the y-axis, θy), plus an angle around the other axes (φ). You can then split the force step-by-step: first into a vertical piece and a horizontal "flat" piece, then split that flat piece further into its own x and z parts.
The idea — Method 2: using "direction cosines" Alternatively, a problem might directly give you the angle the force makes with EACH of the three axes (θx, θy, θz). In that case: Fx=F·cos(θx), Fy=F·cos(θy), Fz=F·cos(θz). These three cosines are called "direction cosines" — they always satisfy the neat rule cos²θx+cos²θy+cos²θz=1, which is a great way to double-check your work.
pg 66–67

The BEST method for 3D forces: using two points along the force's path

The idea — this is the method you'll use the most, so learn it well Very often in 3D problems, we're told a force acts along a wire, rope, or chain connecting two known points (like point A to point B), but we're only given the force's total SIZE (like "the tension is 2500 N"), not the angles. Here's the reliable 3-step method:
  1. Step 1: Find the position vector from A to B: rAB = (xB−xA)i + (yB−yA)j + (zB−zA)k.
  2. Step 2: Find its length (the straight-line distance from A to B), and divide the whole vector by that length to get a pure unit vector — this strips away the distance and keeps only the direction.
  3. Step 3: Multiply that unit vector by the actual force size to get the real force vector: F = F · u.
This "two points → unit vector → multiply by magnitude" recipe works every single time, no matter how complicated the angles look in the picture.

Part 1 — What Is a "Moment"? The Idea of Twisting

pages 70–76
pg 70

A new kind of question: not just "does it move," but "does it TURN?"

The idea In Chapter 2, we treated objects like a single dot (a "particle") and only asked "do all the forces cancel out?" But real objects have SIZE — and a force pushing on one SIDE of an object can make it spin, even if the total push and pull are balanced! Think about pushing on the edge of a door versus pushing right at its hinge — pushing at the edge swings the door open easily, but pushing right at the hinge barely does anything, even with the exact same force. This chapter is all about that "twisting" effect, which we call a moment.

Big idea to hold onto: any messy collection of forces acting on a rigid body can always be replaced by just ONE force plus ONE twist (a "couple"), acting together at a single point. That's the entire goal of this chapter — learning to boil down complicated force systems into that one simple force-plus-twist package.
pg 71

Forces from outside vs. inside (recap), and "equivalent forces"

The idea Quick recap from Chapter 2: external forces come from outside the object (drawn on an FBD); internal forces happen inside, holding the object's own pieces together (usually not drawn).

New idea: two forces are called equivalent if they have the exact same magnitude, same direction, and the same line of action — meaning they cause the exact same overall effect on a rigid body, even if applied at slightly different points along that same line. This connects to the "transmissibility" idea from Chapter 2: sliding a force along its own line doesn't change what it does to the whole rigid body's motion.
pg 72

A brand new kind of multiplication: the "cross product"

The idea — why we need a new tool To measure "how much twist" a force creates, we need a mathematical tool that combines TWO vectors (a position and a force) into a THIRD vector (the twist itself) — and this third vector needs to point along the AXIS the object would spin around, not along either of the original vectors. That special tool is called the cross product, written V = P × Q.
The idea — what the cross product actually does The cross product of two vectors P and Q makes a new vector V with 3 special properties: (1) its size equals PQ·sin(θ), where θ is the angle between P and Q; (2) its direction is exactly perpendicular (a right angle, poking straight out) to the flat plane that contains both P and Q; and (3) you find WHICH way it points using the Right-Hand Rule (point your right hand's fingers along P, then curl them toward Q — your thumb points the direction of V). We'll practice this rule more on page 76.
pg 73

Order matters! And the official definition of a moment

The idea — cross products are NOT like regular multiplication With regular numbers, 3×4 = 4×3 (order doesn't matter). With cross products, order matters a LOT: Q×P = −(P×Q) — swapping the order flips the direction completely backwards! Always be careful about which vector comes first.
The idea — the moment of a force A moment (also called torque) measures how much a force tends to rotate/twist/bend an object around some point. The formula is: M = r × F, where r is a position vector from the pivot point to ANY point along the force's line of action, and F is the force itself.
pg 74

Moment about a point: the full picture

The idea MO = r × F, and its magnitude works out to: MO = r·F·sin(θ) = F·d, where d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot point O to the force's line of action (the shortest, right-angle distance — like measuring how far a wall is from you by walking straight toward it, not diagonally).

The moment vector MO points perpendicular to the flat plane containing both the pivot point and the force — its DIRECTION tells you the axis the object would spin around, and its SIZE tells you how strong that spin tendency is.
pg 75

The simpler 2D version (most of our problems!), and moment arm geometry

The idea — the easy 2D shortcut For flat (2D) problems, we don't need the full cross product — we can just use: MO = F·d (a plain number, called a scalar), where d is again the perpendicular distance. The SIGN tells us the spin direction: positive if it spins counter-clockwise, negative if clockwise (imagine looking at a clock — if the force would push the hands backward, that's counter-clockwise, which we call positive).
The idea — the moment arm doesn't have to be the shortest r Here's a neat fact: you can measure "d" (or use ANY position vector r) from the pivot point to ANY point along the force's full line of action — not just the exact spot where the force touches the object — and you'll always get the same moment. This is because the moment's size is really just the AREA of the parallelogram formed by r and F, and that area stays the same no matter which point along the line you pick.
pg 76

The Right-Hand Rule, explained with your actual hand, and Varignon's Theorem

The idea — Right-Hand Rule, step by step Hold up your right hand. Curl your fingers in the direction the object would spin (say, counter-clockwise). Your thumb, sticking straight out, points in the direction of the moment vector. Try it now with your own hand — curling your right-hand fingers counter-clockwise naturally makes your thumb point UP and OUT toward you. That's the rule, and it always works with your RIGHT hand specifically (not your left!).
The idea — Varignon's Theorem (a very useful shortcut) If several forces all meet at the same point, the moment of their COMBINED resultant force equals the SUM of each individual force's own moment. In simple words: instead of adding up all the forces first and then finding one big moment, you're allowed to find each force's moment separately and add THOSE up — same answer either way, but sometimes one way is much easier math than the other.

Part 3 — Moments in 3D: Using the Cross Product Formula

The "cheat sheet" for i, j, k cross products

The idea Since i, j, k always point perfectly perpendicular to each other, their cross products follow a simple repeating pattern (going "in order" x→y→z→x gives a positive answer; going "backward" gives negative): i×j=k, j×k=i, k×i=j. And going backward flips the sign: j×i=−k, and so on. Also, any vector crossed with ITSELF gives zero (i×i=0) — makes sense, since there's no "twist" if the two vectors point the exact same way.

The determinant trick — a shortcut for computing cross products

The idea — the pattern (don't worry, it's very learnable with practice) To cross-multiply two full 3D vectors, we write them into a 3×3 grid (called a "determinant") with i, j, k on top, then follow a specific criss-cross pattern of multiplying and subtracting to get each of the 3 answer pieces. It looks intimidating the first time, but it's really just a repeatable recipe you can follow every single time — like a recipe card, not something you need to "understand" deeply, just practice.

The formula for the moment of a force about the origin using this method: MO = (yFz−zFy)i + (zFx−xFz)j + (xFy−yFx)k.

Moment about any point (not just the origin), and the easy 2D version again

The idea Everything works the same way if we want the moment about some OTHER point B instead of the origin — we just use rA/B (position of the force's application point, relative to B) instead of a plain position vector from the origin.

For flat (2D) problems, the whole 3D formula collapses down to just one useful number: MO = xFy − yFx — this is the 2D shortcut version of the full cross-product recipe, and it's much faster when everything lies flat in one plane.

The problem A rectangular plate is held by brackets at A and B and a wire CD, with tension 200 N. Position of C relative to A: (0.3, 0.08, 0) m. Wire direction from C to D: (−0.3, 0.24, −0.32) m, total length 0.5 m. Find the moment about A caused by the wire's force at C.
How to solve it Step 1: position vector rC/A = 0.3i + 0.08j (z-part is 0 since C is at the same depth as A).
Step 2: the force vector, using the "unit vector × magnitude" trick from Chapter 2: F = 200 × [(−0.3i+0.24j−0.32k)/0.5] = −120i + 96j − 128k (N).
Step 3: plug r and F into the determinant formula:

M_A = −7.68i + 28.8j + 28.8k (N·m)

Yet another type of multiplication: the "dot product"

The idea — dot product vs. cross product The cross product gives you back a VECTOR (with direction). The dot product gives you back just a plain NUMBER (a scalar) — no direction. Formula: P·Q = PQ·cos(θ). Unlike the cross product, order does NOT matter here (P·Q = Q·P is always true).
The idea — what the dot product is actually used for Two very useful jobs: (1) finding the angle between two vectors — just rearrange the formula to cosθ = (P·Q)/(PQ); and (2) finding the projection of one vector onto a line or axis — basically, "how much of vector P is aimed along that direction?" If you have a unit vector λ pointing along some axis, the projection is simply P·λ.
pg 85

Combining three vectors, and moments about a LINE (not just a point)

The idea — mixed triple product This is a cross product AND a dot product used together: S·(P×Q). The result is just a plain number. This combination is exactly what we need for the next idea.
The idea — moment about a specific axis, not just a point Sometimes we don't care about the FULL twisting effect at a point — we only care how much twist happens around one SPECIFIC line (axis), like a hinge or a rod. The recipe: first find the regular moment about any point O on that axis (MO = r×F, same as before), then take the DOT PRODUCT of that moment with a unit vector pointing along the axis: Maxis = λ · MO = λ · (r × F). This gives you just the "slice" of the twist that's aimed along that one particular line.

Example 5 begins: a force on a cube

The idea before the problem — same rule, applied 4 different ways This example uses the exact same two tools we just learned (M=r×F, and Maxis=λ·M) four separate times, each answering a slightly different question about the same setup. The problem A cube of side length "a" is acted on by force P at corner F, pointing toward B. Find the moment of P: (a) about corner A, (b) about edge AB, (c) about the diagonal AG, (d) the perpendicular distance between lines AG and FC.

Example 5: parts (a), (b), and (c)

Part (a) — moment about corner A Using the position vector from A to F and the force P (written so that its magnitude works out correctly to exactly P), the cross product gives:

M_A = (aP/√2)(i + j + k)
Part (b) — moment about edge AB Edge AB points along the i direction, so we just dot-product with i: M_AB = i · M_A = aP/√2.
Part (c) — moment about the diagonal AG The diagonal's unit vector is λ = (i−j−k)/√3. Dot-producting with M_A: M_AG = (aP/√6)(1−1−1) = −aP/√6.
pg 88

Example 5: part (d), and a brand new idea — "Couples"

Part (d) — perpendicular distance between AG and FC First, a check: P·λ = 0, meaning force P is exactly perpendicular to the diagonal AG. When a force is perpendicular to an axis, the moment magnitude simplifies to just force × distance: |M_AG| = P·d, so aP/√6 = P·d, giving d = a/√6.
The idea — introducing "couples" A couple is a special pair of forces: same size, same line-direction (parallel), but pointing OPPOSITE ways, and NOT on the same line. Picture using both hands to turn a steering wheel — one hand pushes up, the other pushes down, on opposite sides of the wheel. There's no overall push (the forces cancel out for movement purposes) — but there IS a pure twisting effect. The formula for a couple's moment is simply: M = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance BETWEEN the two parallel force lines.

Part 5 — Couples: Twisting Without Pushing

pages 88–92
pg 89

When are two couples "the same," and drawing couples as arrows

The idea — comparing couples Two couples create the exact same twisting effect if F₁×d₁ = F₂×d₂ — even if one uses a bigger force with a smaller arm, and the other uses a smaller force with a bigger arm, as long as the PRODUCT matches, they twist things the same amount. Using a lug wrench to loosen a car's wheel nuts is a real-life couple: your two hands push in opposite directions on the wrench, creating pure twisting.
The idea — couples as "free vectors" We can represent a whole couple with just ONE arrow (a moment vector), pointing along the twist's axis, with length equal to M=Fd. The amazing thing about couple-vectors is that they're free vectors — meaning you can slide them ANYWHERE on the object without changing their effect at all, unlike regular forces which must stay on their own line. This is because a couple doesn't care WHERE on the object it's applied — only how much twist it creates.
pg 90

Sliding a force to a new point (this is where couples come from!)

The idea — Case I: moving a force to a NEW point on the body Here's a clever trick: if we want to move a force from point A to a different point O on the same rigid body, we can't just slide it there for free (unlike sliding along its OWN line, which is fine per transmissibility). Instead, we add a pair of equal-and-opposite forces AT point O (which changes nothing, since they cancel each other out) — and this cleverly creates a leftover COUPLE. So: any force can be replaced by that same force moved to a new point, PLUS a couple that accounts for the "twist" the move creates.
The idea — Case II: what if we move it again, to yet another point? If we then move the force-couple system from point O to a different point O′, the moment changes according to: MO′ = MO + s × F, where s is the position vector from O to O′. In plain words: whenever you relocate a force-couple system to a new reference point, you have to ADD the moment that the force itself would create about that new point.
pg 91

Example 6: combining several couples into one

The problem Determine the single equivalent couple for the couples acting on a bent pipe: 150 N couples with perpendicular arm 0.46 m, and 100 N couples with arms 0.3 m and 0.23 m along different directions.
How to solve it Since couple-vectors are free vectors, we can just add each couple's contribution along each axis separately, exactly like adding regular vector components:

Mx = −(150)(0.46) = −69 N·m   My = +(100)(0.3) = +30 N·m   Mz = +(100)(0.23) = +23 N·m

M = −69i + 30j + 23k (N·m)
pg 92

Double-checking Example 6, and turning any force system into ONE force plus ONE couple

Checking our work with a totally different method The slide double-checks by summing the moments of ALL FOUR individual forces about a single point (D), using the full cross-product method instead — and gets the exact same answer: M = −(69 N·m)i + (30 N·m)j + (23 N·m)k. When two completely different methods agree, that's strong proof the answer is correct.
The idea — reducing ANY force system to one force + one couple This is the big payoff of the whole chapter: no matter how many forces are acting on a rigid body, all at different points, you can always replace the entire mess with just ONE resultant force (R = sum of every force) PLUS ONE resultant couple (MRO = sum of every individual moment about your chosen point O). This "force-couple system" behaves EXACTLY like the original messy pile of forces, for any calculation you'd ever want to do.

Sometimes you can simplify even further — down to just ONE force

The idea If the resultant force R and the resultant couple MRO happen to be perpendicular to each other, you can combine them into a SINGLE force acting along a shifted line (no separate couple needed at all!). Two flavors of "not moving," and the official rules for a rigid body

The idea — static vs. dynamic equilibrium We've used the word "equilibrium" a lot already — it just means "balanced, so nothing changes." There are actually two flavors:
  • Static equilibrium — the object is completely, totally still. Zero speed, zero spin. Examples: a bridge just sitting there, a sign hanging on a wall, a ladder leaning against a wall.
  • Dynamic equilibrium — the object's center is moving, but at a perfectly constant, unchanging speed in a straight line (no speeding up, slowing down, or turning).
Good news: this whole course (and basically every real civil engineering structure you'll design) only cares about static equilibrium — completely still. So from here on, "equilibrium" basically always means "not moving at all."
The idea — the TWO rules a rigid body must obey Remember from Chapter 3: a rigid body has size, so it's not enough for the forces to just cancel out (that only stops it from sliding around) — the TWISTING has to cancel out too (that stops it from spinning). So a rigid body is in equilibrium only when BOTH of these are true at the same time:

ΣF = 0 (all forces added together = zero — no sliding)   AND   ΣMO = 0 (all moments about ANY point added together = zero — no spinning)

Both conditions must hold, and note that if ΣM=0 about ONE point, it will actually be true about EVERY point too (a fact that's very useful, but don't worry about proving it — just trust it and use it).
pg 99

A visual example (balanced rocks), and a "which is it?" thinking question

The idea — the balanced rock photos The slide shows a famous "balanced rock" formation — a huge boulder resting on a tiny point of contact, that still doesn't tip over. If we only cared about the rock as a single dot (a particle), we'd just need ΣF=0 (no net force). But since it's a real, sized rigid body, we ALSO need ΣM=0 — otherwise, even if the total force balances, the rock could still topple over from a twisting effect. This picture is a great reminder of why the "no spinning" rule matters just as much as the "no sliding" rule.
The problem — a thinking question A light (weightless) rod has two forces of EQUAL size acting on it: one pushing down at one end, one pushing up at the other end (like a capital "Z" shape of forces). Which statement is true: is the rod in force equilibrium, torque (moment) equilibrium, both, or neither?
How to think about it Check force balance first: one force pushes up, the other pushes down, and they're equal size — so yes, ΣF=0 (force equilibrium ✓). Now check moment balance: even though the forces cancel out for sliding purposes, they're NOT on the same line — one is on the left end, one is on the right end. This is exactly a "couple" from Chapter 3! A couple creates a pure twist that never cancels to zero just because the forces are equal and opposite. So the rod is in force equilibrium, but NOT in torque (moment) equilibrium — it would spin, even though it wouldn't slide anywhere.

A classic seesaw example, and starting our first real FBD for this chapter

The idea — the seesaw A seesaw is one of the simplest, most familiar examples of moment balance. Two people sit on opposite sides of a see-saw's pivot; for it to balance perfectly (not tip either way), their moments about the pivot must be exactly equal: 800 N × 3 m = 400 N × 6 m (both sides equal 2400 N·m). Notice the person with LESS weight (400 N) can still balance the heavier person (800 N) by sitting FARTHER from the pivot — that's the whole idea of "moment = force × distance" showing up in a playground.
The idea — building a Free Body Diagram for a real structure The slide introduces a crane-like truss structure holding a 2400 kg load, supported by a pin at one point (A) and a roller at another (B). This is the FIRST step of solving ANY equilibrium problem for a rigid body:
  1. Isolate the whole structure by itself, detached from the ground and anything else touching it.
  2. Replace the structure's own weight with a single downward arrow at its center of gravity (labeled G).
  3. Replace every support with the correct reaction force(s) it can actually provide (we'll learn exactly which supports give which reactions very soon).
  4. Include all the measurement distances you'll need for the moment calculations.
This exact FBD (the crane holding 2400 kg) is the very first full worked example of the next part of Chapter 4 — where we'll actually solve for the unknown support reactions using both ΣF=0 and ΣM=0 together. That's where we'll pick back up.
