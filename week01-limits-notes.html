What is a limit, really?

Sneaking up on a number, what happens when the two sides don't agree, and the tricks for actually computing an answer.

page 02

Section 10.1 & 10.2Sneaking up on a number

Imagine you're walking toward a puddle, getting closer and closer, but you're not allowed to actually step in it. A limit is exactly that idea, but for numbers on a graph: as x tip-toes closer and closer to some number (here, 1), we watch what height (y-value) the curve is tip-toeing toward.

In the picture, as x sneaks up on 1 from both sides, the curve's height sneaks up on 2 — even though there's a little hole right at x=1 itself. We don't care what's happening exactly at x=1. We only care where it's headed. Math people write that like this:

limx→1 f(x) = 2

Say it out loud as: "as x heads toward 1, f(x) heads toward 2."

page 03

The grown-up version of the same idea

Page 2 was one example. This page just says the same "sneaking up" idea works for any number a, and any target L: if we can make f(x) sit as close as we want to L (and stay there) just by pushing x close enough to a, then L is the limit.

And here's the important warning: sometimes there's no number the curve settles on — it can't decide, or it runs off to nowhere. When that happens, we just shrug and say the limit "does not exist," which everyone shortens to DNE.

Sneaking up from just one side

Sometimes the left path and the right path lead to different places — like two hiking trails up the same mountain that end at different peaks. This graph looks like a bent "V": coming in from the left, the height heads toward −1; coming in from the right, it heads toward 1.

limx→0− f(x) = −1
limx→0+ f(x) = 1

The tiny − means "sneaking in from the left/below." The tiny + means "sneaking in from the right/above." That's the whole secret code.

page 06

When the two sides can't agree

Same picture as page 5: the left path wants −1, the right path wants 1. Since they can't agree on one number, the (regular, two-sided) limit doesn't exist. We write DNE, which just stands for "Does Not Exist" — like asking two friends where to eat and getting two completely different answers.

Remember thisA limit — when it exists — can only ever point to one number. The moment you find two different numbers, you already know the answer is DNE. No more work needed!
page 07

The official rulebook version

This page just writes pages 5–6's idea as an official rule you can quote:

limx→a f(x) = L   exactly when   limx→a− f(x) = L  and  limx→a+ f(x) = L

In plain words: check the left path and the right path separately. If they land on the same number, that's your answer. If they land on different numbers, the answer is DNE. That's the whole recipe used for the rest of this course.

The "legal move" rulebook

Once you know two limits separately, these seven rules let you combine them without redoing all the sneaking-up work. Think of it like LEGO instructions: if you already know two pieces individually, you're allowed to snap them together.

  • Adding or subtracting two limits? Add or subtract the answers.
  • Multiplying by a plain number? Multiply the answer by that number.
  • Multiplying or dividing two limits? Multiply or divide the answers (dividing needs the bottom answer to not be zero).
  • Raising to a power, or taking a root? Do that same thing to the answer.

In short: limits play nice with +, −, ×, ÷, and powers, in exactly the way you'd hope. This is why "just plug in the number" so often works.

Why "nice" functions are always this easy

A polynomial is just a function built purely from adding, subtracting, multiplying by numbers, and whole-number powers of x — like 3x⁴−6x²+x−10. Because those are exactly the "legal moves" from page 11, plugging in the number ALWAYS works for polynomials. No exceptions, ever.

Example 5 — a function that changes its rule partway

f(x) = x³ − 2x + 5,  when |x| ≤ 2(x+7)/(x−1),  when |x| > 2

Both x=2 and x=−2 sit exactly on the seam where the rule changes. So we have to check the left path and right path separately at each seam, using whichever rule applies on that side.

At x = 2
  1. Coming from the left (just under 2, so the top rule applies): 2³−2(2)+5 = 8−4+5 = 9.
  2. Coming from the right (just over 2, so the bottom rule applies): (2+7)/(2−1) = 9.
  3. Both sides say 9 — they agree!
limx→2 f(x) = 9
At x = −2
  1. Coming from the left (just under −2, so |x|>2, bottom rule): (−2+7)/(−2−1) = 5/(−3) = −5/3.
  2. Coming from the right (just over −2, so |x|<2, top rule): (−2)³−2(−2)+5 = −8+4+5 = 1.
  3. −5/3 and 1 don't match — the two sides disagree.
limx→−2 f(x) = DNE

Example 6 — when plugging in gives a broken 0/0

limx→−1 x² − 1x² − 3x − 4
Solution
  1. Plug in x=−1: you get 0/0. That's not a real answer — it's a clue that says "the top and bottom secretly share a hidden matching piece; go factor them and cancel it out."
  2. Factor: x²−1 = (x−1)(x+1), and x²−3x−4 = (x−4)(x+1).
  3. Cancel the shared (x+1) piece (this is allowed because we only ever look at x near −1, never exactly at it): left with (x−1)/(x−4).
  4. Now plug in x=−1 safely: (−1−1)/(−1−4) = −2/−5.
2/5

Example 8 — the "multiply by the twin" trick

limx→0 √x+4 − 2x² − x

The square root is on top this time, so factoring won't reach it. Instead we multiply top and bottom by the square root's "twin" (same numbers, flipped sign in the middle) — this makes the square root vanish through a difference-of-squares trick.

Solution
  1. Multiply by √(x+4)+2√(x+4)+2: on top, (x+4)−4 = x.
  2. The bottom also has a shared piece: x²−x = x(x−1). So now we have xx(x−1)[√(x+4)+2].
  3. Cancel the shared x: left with 1(x−1)[√(x+4)+2].
  4. Now plug in x=0: (0−1)[√4+2] = (−1)(2+2) = −4.
−1/4

Example 9 — absolute value bars force a two-path check

limx→3 |x² − x − 6|x − 3

Absolute-value bars |·| mean "make it positive, whatever it was." That's itself a "changes its rule" situation, so any time |·| wraps something that flips sign exactly where we're zooming in, we need to check left and right separately — just like Example 5.

Solution
  1. Factor what's inside the bars: x²−x−6 = (x−3)(x+2). Near x=3, (x+2) is close to 5, always positive, so we can leave that part alone.
  2. The (x−3) part is what flips sign: for x just above 3, |x−3|/(x−3) = +1. For x just below 3, it equals −1.
  3. Right side (x→3+): (+1)·(x+2) → 1·5 = 5.
  4. Left side (x→3−): (−1)·(x+2) → −1·5 = −5.
  5. 5 and −5 don't match.
DNE (left side gives −5, right side gives 5)

When a limit runs off to infinity

Sometimes instead of heading toward a nice number, a function just keeps climbing (or falling) forever. Here's how to spot it, and how to prove it.

page 20

Two different questions, one symbol (∞)

The word "infinity" (∞) shows up in two totally different questions from here on, so let's tell them apart right away:

Infinite limitsWe stand still at one x-spot and ask: does the height shoot up (or down) forever as we get close?
Limits at infinityWe ask: what does the height do way, way out at the far edges, as x itself grows huge?
page 21

A real example, from the textbook

Take 2/(x+1) and creep toward x=−1 from the right (try x=−0.9, then −0.99, then −0.999…). The bottom (x+1) shrinks toward 0 but always stays a tiny positive number. Dividing 2 by a tinier and tinier positive number makes the answer bigger and bigger — with absolutely no ceiling.

limx→−1+ 2x+1 = ∞
limx→−1− 2x+1 = −∞

Coming from the left, (x+1) shrinks toward 0 but stays tiny negative — so 2 divided by that is a giant negative number instead. Writing "= ∞" is really just a fancy way of saying "runs away forever," not a real number you land on.

page 22

The two-step trick this whole course uses

Instead of reasoning it out fresh every single time, here's a shortcut recipe for proving a limit runs off to ∞ or −∞:

Step 1Flip the function upside-down (take 1/f(x)) and check that that limit is 0. This proves f(x) itself is blowing up in size.
Step 2Check the sign of f(x) right near that spot. Positive nearby ⇒ it's shooting up to ∞. Negative nearby ⇒ it's diving down to −∞.

Step 1 tells you it's getting huge. Step 2 tells you which direction "huge" points.

Example 10 — using the two-step trick

limx→1 x(x²−1)²   and   limx→−1 x(x²−1)²
At x = 1
  1. Step 1: flip it: (x²−1)²/x. Plug in x=1: (1−1)²/1 = 0. Confirmed, it's blowing up.
  2. Step 2: near x=1, the top (x) is close to 1 (positive), and the bottom is something squared, so it's always positive. Positive over positive is positive.

At x = −1
  1. Step 1: flip it: ((−1)²−1)²/(−1) = 0/(−1) = 0. Confirmed.
  2. Step 2: near x=−1, the top is close to −1 (negative); the bottom is still squared, so still positive. Negative over positive is negative.

What happens way, way out at the edges

Instead of zooming in on one x-spot, now we zoom out — what does a function do when x is a giant number, positive or negative?

page 28

The building blocks for "way out there"

  1. xn runs off to ∞ in both directions when n is an even number (like x² — both ends point up).
  2. xn runs off to +∞ on the right but −∞ on the left when n is odd (like x³ — the two ends point opposite ways).
  3. 1/xn always shrinks down to 0 way out at either edge (a giant number on the bottom makes the whole fraction tiny).

For a polynomial (a sum of x-powers), only the single biggest power matters way out at the edges — every smaller term becomes too tiny to matter by comparison. So a polynomial's far-out behavior is decided entirely by its leading (highest-power) term.

Example 13(a) — 3x⁴ − 6x² + x − 10

Biggest term: 3x⁴. Power 4 is even, and the number in front (+3) is positive, so both ends point up.

x→∞: +∞  ·  x→−∞: +∞
Example 13(b) — −2x³ + 3x² − 12

Biggest term: −2x³. Power 3 is odd (so the ends normally point opposite ways), and the negative sign in front flips both of those directions.

x→∞: −∞  ·  x→−∞: +∞
page 29

Example 14 — a fraction with x's on top AND bottom

limx→∞ 4x² + 52x² + 1

Both the top and bottom race off to ∞ here, so plugging in doesn't help (∞/∞ isn't a real number). The trick: divide every single term, top and bottom, by the biggest power of x in the bottom (here, x²).

Why this works
  1. Dividing everything by x² turns the top into 4 + 5/x², and the bottom into 2 + 1/x².
  2. Way out at x→∞, every "something/xpower" term shrinks to 0, leaving only the plain numbers: (4+0)/(2+0).
4/2 = 2 (same answer heading toward −∞, too)

Picture it this way: the curve flattens out and hugs the flat line y=2 way out on both edges — that flat line is called a horizontal asymptote, the "way out there" cousin of page 24's vertical walls.

Example 15 — same trick, different ending

  2. Every piece of the new top shrinks to 0 way out at −∞, so the whole top → 0. The bottom → 1.

Before you start: 4 quick tools Week 2 assumes you already have

Week 2 uses these constantly without re-explaining them. If any of these four feel shaky, read this box first — everything below will make much more sense.

1. Factoring — "undo-ing" a multiplication

Factoring just means: given something like x²+3x−4, find two simpler pieces that multiply back together to give it. The trick for x²+bx+c: find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

Example: x²+3x−4. Which two numbers multiply to −4 and add to 3? Try 4 and −1: 4×(−1)=−4 ✓, and 4+(−1)=3 ✓. So x²+3x−4 = (x+4)(x−1). That's it — you're just un-doing FOIL.

2. Why you can NEVER divide by zero

Try 5÷0 on a calculator — it errors out. There's no number that makes sense as an answer. So any time the bottom of a fraction would become 0 at some x-value, that x-value gets banned from the domain — the function simply isn't defined there, full stop. This single fact is why continuity (page 8) always checks "is the bottom zero?" first, and why sign-chart inequalities (page 17 onward) always throw out the bottom's zeros.

3. Exponent (power) shortcuts, for the power rule

The power rule (page 32) needs you to see EVERY expression as "x to some power," including these disguises:

4. The point-slope line formula

If you know one point (x1,y1) a line passes through, and you know its slope m, you can always write the line's equation as: y − y1 = m(x − x1). Example: slope 6, through (1,4): y−4 = 6(x−1), which tidies up to y=6x−2. This is exactly the last step of every tangent-line problem from page 27 onward — the derivative just hands you the m, and you already know the point.

page 02

The cheat-sheet for "top power vs. bottom power"

This page is the grown-up, official version of the pattern we noticed at the end of Week 1 (page 30). Call the biggest power on top "m" and the biggest power on bottom "n." Then, way out at x→±∞:

  • Same power (m=n): the answer is just the front numbers divided — top's leading number over bottom's leading number.
  • Top smaller (m<n): the answer is always 0 — the bottom wins the race, easily.
  • Top bigger (m>n): the answer runs off to ∞ or −∞ — the top wins the race, and you have to check the sign to know which one.

Memorize this table and you can often tell the shape of the answer before doing a single line of algebra.

No graph? Just plug in a huge number instead You don't need a picture to feel why this table is true — a giant number does the job. Try x = 1000 in each case:
  1. Same power — (4x²+5)/(2x²+1): top ≈ 4,000,005, bottom ≈ 2,000,001. The "+5" and "+1" are peanuts next to the millions, so the ratio is basically 4,000,000/2,000,000 = 2, just the leading numbers.
  2. Bottom bigger — (3x³−7)/(x⁴+5x²): top ≈ 3 billion, bottom ≈ 1 trillion. 3 billion split across 1 trillion is a tiny sliver, and it keeps shrinking as x grows — heading to 0.
  3. Top bigger — (x⁵−4x³+2x−1)/(x³−7x): top ≈ 1 quadrillion, bottom ≈ 1 billion. A quadrillion over a billion is already about a million, and it only grows from there — no ceiling, so it's ∞.

The rule underneath all three: once x is huge, only the very biggest power in the top and the very biggest power in the bottom matter at all — everything else is rounding error by comparison.

Example 17 — top power bigger, so it runs to infinity

limx→∞ x⁵ − 4x³ + 2x − 1x³ − 7x

Top power is 5, bottom power is 3 — top is bigger, so by the cheat-sheet on page 2, this must run off to ±∞. We just need Week 1's two-step method (page 22) to pin down the sign.

Solution

Continuity — can you draw it without lifting your pencil?

A function is "continuous" at a point if its graph has no hole, no jump, and no break there — you could trace it with a pencil and never lift it up.

page 07

Section 10.3Meet continuity

Here's the big shortcut this whole idea unlocks: for LOTS of functions, finding the limit is as easy as just calculating f at that number — no tricks, no sneaking up needed. Functions where this shortcut works are called continuous there.

Picture it like a pencil test: the left graph never lifts the pencil, so it's continuous. The right graph has a break (the pencil has to jump), so it's not continuous there.

page 08

The official 3-item checklist

To officially call f "continuous at a," all three of these have to be true — miss even one, and it's not continuous there (that spot is then called a "point of discontinuity").

1. f(a) actually exists (a is allowed in the domain — no dividing by zero, etc.)
2. The limit as x→a exists (the left path and right path agree on a number)
3. That limit actually equals f(a) — the curve is heading exactly where the dot really is

Notice: condition 2, written out, is exactly the left-limit-equals-right-limit rule from Week 1 page 7. Continuity is really just "limit exists AND matches the actual dot."

Try it yourself — walk the checklist on f(x) = (x²−9)/(x−3) at x=3

  1. Rule 1: plug x=3 into the bottom: 3−3=0. Dividing by zero is banned (refresher #2 above) — so f(3) is undefined. Rule 1 already fails.
  2. Since Rule 1 already broke, we don't even need to check Rules 2 and 3 — one broken rule is enough to call it quits.
Not continuous at x = 3 (f(3) doesn't exist)

Two free passes: polynomials and fractions of polynomials

a. A polynomial (built only from +, −, ×, and whole-number powers of x) is continuous everywhere, no exceptions.
b. A fraction of two polynomials (like x/(x²−7x+12)) is continuous everywhere except where the bottom would be zero.

These two facts are why "just plug in the number" works so often — and they instantly tell you where the trouble spots can hide (only ever at the zeros of a denominator).

More free passes: combining continuous functions

Section 10.4Using continuity to solve "greater than / less than" problems

New skill unlocked by continuity: solving inequalities like x²+3x−4 > 0, without testing every single number one at a time.

The textbook's walkthrough
  1. Let f(x) = x²+3x−4, and factor it: (x+4)(x−1).
  2. f is a polynomial, so (page 12) it's continuous everywhere — meaning its graph never secretly jumps over the x-axis without touching it.
  3. f(x)=0 exactly at the roots x=−4 and x=1. Those two special numbers slice the whole number line into three neighborhoods: (−∞,−4), (−4,1), and (1,∞).
page 17

The clever part: one test point tells you the WHOLE neighborhood

Here's the magic trick continuity gives us: inside one of those three neighborhoods (say, between −4 and 1), the curve can never sneak from positive to negative without physically crossing zero somewhere along the way — because there's no gap to jump through, the curve is continuous! And we already know the only zeros are at −4 and 1, the neighborhood's edges. So the sign can't change anywhere inside that neighborhood.

That means testing just one single point inside a neighborhood tells you the sign for the entire neighborhood. No need to check a hundred numbers — just one per neighborhood!

Section 11.1What even IS a tangent line?

Big scene change! From here on, calculus asks a brand new question: if you zoom in on a curve at one exact point, what's the slope (steepness) of the curve right there? That steepness is measured by a special line called the tangent line — the line that just barely "kisses" the curve at that one spot and points in the exact same direction the curve is going.

The picture shows why "a line touching the curve at just one point" isn't a good enough definition: L1 really is tangent at P, but L2 also only touches at one point and it's clearly cutting across, not hugging the curve. Meanwhile L3 is tangent at P even though the wiggly curve happens to touch that same flat line again somewhere far away. Tangent-ness is about matching direction right at that one spot, not about counting touches.

page 23

Building the tangent line out of "almost tangent" lines

Here's the clever idea: first draw an easy line — a secant line, which is just any line that crosses the curve at two points (say P and another point Q). Now imagine sliding Q closer and closer to P along the curve. The secant line rotates as Q slides in, and it settles into one final resting direction right as Q reaches P. That resting direction is defined to be the tangent line.

page 24

Turning "sliding closer" into an actual formula

Remember "rise over run" from algebra? That's all a secant line's slope is: (rise)÷(run), between two points on the curve. If our two points are (a, f(a)) and (z, f(z)), then rise = f(z)−f(a), and run = z−a. So the secant slope is just:

f(z) − f(a)z − a

Now here's the one new idea on this whole page: slide z in toward a (exactly like Week 1!). That turns our plain slope formula into a limit — and whatever number that limit settles on IS the tangent line's slope.

mtan = limz→a f(z) − f(a)z − a = limh→0 f(a+h) − f(a)h

The second version says the exact same thing in different handwriting: h is just a nickname for the little gap "z−a", so z=a+h. Since h→0 means "the gap is shrinking to nothing," it's the same sliding-closer idea as z→a. We'll use the h version from now on because it's easier to do algebra with — you'll see why in the very next example.

page 25

The derivative — finally, the star of the show

Page 24 found the tangent slope at one frozen number, a. This page just asks: what if we don't freeze it — what if we let it work at any x we want? Swap every "a" for an "x" in the formula, and you get a brand new function that spits out a slope for whatever x you feed it. That new function has a name: f prime, written f′.

f′(x) = limh→0 f(x+h) − f(x)h

Think of f′ as a "steepness calculator": plug in any x, and it hands back the slope of the tangent line at that exact spot. Finding f′ is called differentiation — same root word as "differ," because you're literally looking at the difference f(x+h)−f(x) up in that formula.

Last thing, purely about notation: people write "the derivative" a bunch of different ways that all mean the identical thing — dy/dx, d/dx(f(x)), y′, Dxy. It's just different handwriting habits from different textbooks, nothing mathematically different.

Cute mnemonic from the slide: f(x) is the coffee beans; f′(x) is the brewed cup of coffee — the derivative is what you get after "processing" the original function.

A "for-free" fact: having a derivative means being continuous

"Differentiable at a" is just the official word for "f′(a) exists" — the tangent line at a is well-defined. The theorem says: whenever that's true, f is automatically continuous at a too (no gaps, no jumps). That makes sense — you can't measure a clean, single steepness at a spot where the graph is literally torn apart.

The version you'll actually useFlip that sentence backwards and it becomes a genuinely useful shortcut: if you can see a break in the graph at some point, you already know — instantly, with zero extra work — that there's no tangent line possible there either. Spot a hole or a jump? Skip straight to "not differentiable," no need to check anything else.

Derivatives in real life: how fast is it changing right now?

A tangent line's slope isn't just a geometry fact — in the real world it means "the instant rate of change." Speed, growth, price, volume — anything that changes has a derivative describing how fast.

page 34

Section 11.3The derivative's second meaning: speed

So far, a derivative has meant "slope of the tangent line" (a geometry idea). Here's a totally different-feeling meaning for the exact same math: if s=f(t) tracks an object's position over time t, then the average speed over a time interval is just distance-traveled ÷ time-taken (nothing new — that's how average speed always works). The speed at one exact instant is what you get by shrinking that time interval down to basically nothing — which is exactly a derivative, ds/dt.

vaverage = f(t+Δt) − f(t)Δt     vinstant = limΔt→0 f(t+Δt) − f(t)Δt = ds/dt

A handy shortcut: using the derivative to guess a small change

Because Δy/Δx is close to dy/dx when Δx is small, we can flip that around into a quick prediction tool: Δy ≈ (dy/dx)·Δx. In plain words: multiply the derivative by however much x moved, and you get a good guess for how much y moved.

Special case worth memorizingIf x changes by exactly 1 (one whole unit — like "one more item made," or "one more year passed"), then the change in y is approximately just dy/dx itself. This is exactly the idea behind "marginal" anything in business — marginal cost, marginal revenue — which we'll meet very soon.

PAGES 2–9

Marginal cost and marginal revenue

"Marginal" is just business-speak for "the derivative" — here's why that word choice actually makes sense.

page 02

Meet the cost function

Picture a lemonade stand. Building the stand costs $500 up front — you pay that once, no matter how many cups you end up selling. Then each cup of lemonade costs $0.10 in sugar and lemons — that part scales with how many cups you make. Add those two pieces together and you get a cost function, C(x): the total cost of making the first x items.

C(x) = 500 + 0.1x

The $500 is called the fixed cost (paid once, no matter what — setup, rent, equipment). The $0.1 per item is the variable cost (also called the unit cost) — it multiplies by however many items you make.

page 03

Average cost — spreading the fixed cost around

If it costs C(x) total to make x items, then the average cost per item is just C(x)÷x. For our lemonade stand:

C(x)x = 500 + 0.1xx = 500x + 0.1

So the average cost of the first 1000 cups is C(1000)/1000 = $600/1000 = $0.60 per cup. As x grows, the 500/x piece keeps shrinking — the $500 setup cost gets spread across more and more cups, so each cup's average share of it gets cheaper and cheaper. That's exactly a limit-at-infinity question from Week 1!

limx→∞ C(x)x = 500x + 0.1 → 0 + 0.1 = 0.1

In plain words: if you sold an enormous, unlimited number of cups, the average cost per cup would settle right down to just the $0.10 variable cost — the fixed cost becomes irrelevant per-cup once you spread it far enough.

page 04

Marginal cost — the cost of just ONE more item

Average cost answers "what did the items I've already made cost me, on average?" But there's a different, often more useful question: having already made x items, what does it cost to make Δx more? That extra cost is C(x+Δx)−C(x), so the cost per extra item is:

C(x+Δx) − C(x)Δx = ΔCΔx

That's just a difference quotient — the exact same shape we built derivatives out of back in Week 2! Let Δx shrink to 0, and:

limΔx→0 ΔCΔx = C′(x)

This is called the marginal cost. One honest wrinkle: in real life Δx can't actually shrink to 0 — you can't sell a fraction of a cup. But C′(x) turns out to be a great approximation for the cost of exactly one more item, which is why business people use it anyway.

page 05

Why C′(x) approximates "one more item"

Average cost C̄(x) = C(x)/x — the cost per item, averaged over everything made so far.
Marginal cost C′(x) — the approximate cost of producing just one more item, after having made x.

The slide asks: can you see why C′(x) can be read that way? Here's the connection — remember Week 2 page 38's rule, "if x changes by exactly 1, the change in y is approximately dy/dx"? Producing one more item is exactly Δx=1, so ΔC ≈ C′(x)·1 = C′(x). That's the whole trick: marginal cost is just that one-unit-change approximation, applied to cost.

Notation heads-up: the textbook often uses q (for "quantity") instead of x from here on — same idea, different letter.

Same idea, but for money coming IN: marginal revenue

Product Rule and Quotient Rule

Section 11.4 Two new shortcut rules for when functions get multiplied or divided together — because, surprisingly, you can't just multiply (or divide) their derivatives.

page 10

The tempting shortcut that doesn't work

Sum and difference play nice with derivatives (Week 2 page 32): d/dx[f+g] = f′+g′. So it's tempting to guess the same works for multiplication: d/dx[fg] = f′g′. Let's test that guess with f(x)=x³ and g(x)=x⁴.

The real answer: fg = x³·x⁴ = x⁵, so d/dx(fg) = 7x⁶.

The tempting (wrong) guess: f′g′ = 3x²·4x³ = 12x⁵.

7x⁶ and 12x⁵ are nowhere close — so the guess is busted. Multiplication (and, it turns out, division) needs its own dedicated rule.

Product Rule   d/dx[f(x)g(x)] = f′(x)g(x) + f(x)g′(x)

In words: (derivative of the first)×(the second, untouched), PLUS (the first, untouched)×(derivative of the second). Every product-rule problem is exactly this pattern, twice.

page 11

Watching the product rule in action

F(x) = (x²+3x)(4x+5)
Solution
  1. Name the two pieces: f(x)=x²+3x (so f′(x)=2x+3), and g(x)=4x+5 (so g′(x)=4).
  2. Apply the pattern: F′(x) = f′(x)g(x) + f(x)g′(x) = (2x+3)(4x+5) + (x²+3x)(4).
  3. Expand (2x+3)(4x+5) = 8x²+10x+12x+15 = 8x²+22x+15.
  4. Expand (x²+3x)(4) = 4x²+12x.
  5. Add them together: 8x²+22x+15+4x²+12x = 12x²+34x+15.
F′(x) = 12x² + 34x + 15
Double-check, the slow wayYou could also just multiply (x²+3x)(4x+5) out first into a single polynomial, THEN use the ordinary power rule term by term — you'd land on the exact same 12x²+34x+15. The product rule is really just a shortcut that skips the expanding step.
page 12

The Quotient Rule, and Example 38

Quotient Rule   d/dxf(x)g(x) = g(x)f′(x) − f(x)g′(x)(g(x))²

A classic memory trick: calling the top "hi" and the bottom "lo," this reads as "lo·d(hi) minus hi·d(lo), all over lo·lo." Notice the order matters here (unlike the product rule) — and watch the minus sign; it's the single most common place to slip up.

Find the derivative of f(x) = x³ − 4x² + xx − 2
Solution
  1. Top is "hi": x³−4x²+x, so hi′ = 3x²−8x+1. Bottom is "lo": x−2, so lo′=1.
  2. lo·hi′ = (x−2)(3x²−8x+1) = 3x³−8x²+x−6x²+16x−2 = 3x³−14x²+17x−2.
  3. hi·lo′ = (x³−4x²+x)(1) = x³−4x²+x.
  4. Subtract: (3x³−14x²+17x−2) − (x³−4x²+x) = 2x³−10x²+16x−2.
  5. Divide by lo² = (x−2)².
f′(x) = (2x³ − 10x² + 16x − 2) / (x−2)²
Try it yourself — differentiate f(x) = (2x²+1)/(x+3)

Same 5 moves as Example 38 above: name hi and lo, find hi′ and lo′, then lo·hi′ − hi·lo′, all over lo².

  1. hi=2x²+1, hi′=4x. lo=x+3, lo′=1.
  2. lo·hi′ = (x+3)(4x) = 4x²+12x.
  3. hi·lo′ = (2x²+1)(1) = 2x²+1.
  4. Subtract: (4x²+12x) − (2x²+1) = 2x²+12x−1.
f′(x) = (2x² + 12x − 1) / (x+3)²

We're never told what g(x) actually is as a formula — just its value and its derivative, both at x=4. That's completely fine: the product rule only ever needs values plugged in at the end, so we can plug in numbers instead of formulas the whole way through.

Solution
  1. Let the "first" piece be √x (so its derivative, by the power rule on x1/2, is 1/(2√x)), and the "second" piece be g(x).
  2. Product rule: f′(x) = [1/(2√x)]·g(x) + √x·g′(x).
  3. Plug in x=4 everywhere, using √4=2: f′(4) = [1/(2·2)]·g(4) + 2·g′(4) = (1/4)(8) + 2(7).
  4. (1/4)(8) = 2, and 2(7) = 14.

The Chain Rule

Section 11.5 The single most-used shortcut in calculus — for whenever one function is nested inside another, like a set of Russian nesting dolls.

page 15

Meet the Chain Rule

Some functions are really two functions stacked inside each other — an "outer" function wrapped around an "inner" one. Write the inner piece as u=g(x), and the outer piece as y=f(u). The Chain Rule says:

Version 1   dydx = dydu · dudx
Version 2   d/dx[f(g(x))] = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)

Both versions say the identical thing: differentiate the outside (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Think of it like peeling an onion, one layer at a time, from the outside in — and multiplying a new derivative on with each layer you peel.

page 16

Example 40 — chain rule with a formula for u already given

y = ∛u − u,   u = x²+22x−5. Find dy/dx at x=1.
Solution
  1. Rewrite ∛u as u1/3, so y=u1/3−u. Power rule: dy/du = (1/3)u−2/3 − 1.
  2. Find du/dx with the quotient rule on u=(x²+2)/(2x−5): du/dx = [(2x−5)(2x) − (x²+2)(2)] / (2x−5)² = (2x²−10x−4)/(2x−5)².
  3. Now plug in x=1. First find u itself: u = (1+2)/(2−5) = 3/(−3) = −1.
  4. du/dx at x=1: (2−10−4)/(2−5)² = (−12)/9 = −4/3.
  5. dy/du at u=−1: (1/3)(−1)−2/3 − 1. Since (−1)1/3=−1, squaring gives (−1)2/3=1, so (−1)−2/3=1/1=1. So dy/du = (1/3)(1) − 1 = −2/3.
  6. Chain rule: dy/dx = (dy/du)·(du/dx) = (−2/3)·(−4/3).
dy/dx at x=1 is 8/9

Example 41 — the classic "something to a power" case

y = (5x+4)³
Solution

  2. Chain rule: dy/dx = (dy/du)·(du/dx) = 3u²·5.
  3. Swap u back for 5x+4: = 3(5x+4)²·5.
dy/dx = 15(5x+4)²

Notice the (5x+4)² is just left alone, untouched, exactly as it was — the chain rule never asks you to expand what's inside the parentheses.

Example 43 — product rule AND chain rule, working together

Show that d/dx(x²√(x²+1)) = 3x³+2x√(x²+1)

This is a product of two pieces (x² and √(x²+1)) — so start with the product rule. But the second piece is itself "something inside a square root," so finding its derivative needs the chain rule too. Both rules, one problem.

Solution
  1. Name the pieces: f=x² (so f′=2x), and g=√(x²+1)=(x²+1)1/2.
  2. Find g′ with the chain rule (general power rule): g′ = (1/2)(x²+1)−1/2·2x = x/√(x²+1).
  3. Product rule: f′g + fg′ = 2x√(x²+1) + x²·[x/√(x²+1)] = 2x√(x²+1) + x³/√(x²+1).
  4. Combine over one common denominator, √(x²+1): the first term becomes 2x(x²+1)/√(x²+1), since √(x²+1)·√(x²+1)=x²+1.
  5. Add the numerators: 2x(x²+1) + x³ = 2x³+2x+x³ = 3x³+2x.

Example 44 — peeling three layers of onion

Nothing stops the chain rule from applying more than once — every extra layer of nesting just means one more multiplication.

y = √(x + √(x + √x))
Peel one layer at a time, from the inside out
  1. Innermost layer: A = √x = x1/2. A′ = 1/(2√x).
  2. Next layer out: B = x + A. B′ = 1 + A′ = 1 + 1/(2√x).
  3. Next layer: C = √B. By the chain rule, C′ = B′ / (2√B) = [1 + 1/(2√x)] / (2√(x+√x)).
  4. Next layer: D = x + C. D′ = 1 + C′.
  5. Outermost layer (this is y itself): y = √D. By the chain rule, y′ = D′ / (2√D).
y′ = 1 + C′2√(x+√(x+√x)),  where C′ = 1 + 1/(2√x)2√(x+√x)

Mental break — a joke that's secretly a hint

"Not sure if I should use the chain rule or the power rule"

More business applications

Marginal propensity to consume/save, and a big multi-step example that uses the chain rule, quotient rule, and power rule all in one problem.

page 22

The consumption function — how a country spends its income

For a whole country, let I = total national income and C = total national consumption (spending), both usually in billions of dollars. The marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is just dC/dI — of every extra dollar of income, how many cents get spent.

MPC = dCdI

Whatever isn't spent is saved: savings S = I − C. Differentiating both sides with respect to I:

dSdI = 1 − dCdI

dS/dI is called the marginal propensity to save (MPS). In plain words: every extra dollar of income gets split between spending and saving, so MPC and MPS always add up to exactly 1 (the whole dollar) — MPS = 1 − MPC.

page 23

Example 45 — finding MPC and MPS

C = 5(2√(I³) + 3)I + 10,   find MPC and MPS at I=100
Solution
  1. Clean up first: √(I³) = I3/2, so C = (10I3/2+15)/(I+10) — a quotient, so this needs the quotient rule.
  2. hi = 10I3/2+15, hi′ = 15I1/2 = 15√I. lo = I+10, lo′=1.
  3. At I=100: √I=10, so I3/2=1000. hi = 10(1000)+15=10015. hi′ = 15(10)=150. lo=110.
  4. Quotient rule: dC/dI = (lo·hi′ − hi·lo′)/lo² = (110·150 − 10015·1)/110² = (16500−10015)/12100 = 6485/12100.
MPC(100) = 6485/12100 ≈ 0.536
Now MPS — no new work needed
  1. MPS = 1 − MPC = 1 − 0.536.
MPS(100) ≈ 0.464

In plain words: when national income is around 100 (billion), roughly 53.6¢ of every extra dollar earned gets spent, and the remaining 46.4¢ gets saved.

Marginal-revenue product — a chain rule in disguise

Suppose a manufacturer hires m employees, who together produce q units per day — so q depends on m. Selling those q units brings in revenue r — so r depends on q. That means r secretly depends on m too, just through q — exactly the "function nested inside a function" setup the chain rule was built for.

drdm = drdq · dqdm

dr/dm is called the marginal-revenue product — the approximate extra revenue from hiring one more employee.

Example 46 — the big one, part 1: setting up dr/dq

q = 10m²√(m²+19),   demand equation p = 900/(q+9).   Find the marginal-revenue product at m=9.

Four numbers, three formulas, two rules — the trick to not getting lost is to work it in clearly separated stages. Stage 1: find dr/dq.

Build the revenue function, then differentiate it
  1. Revenue = price × quantity: r = pq = [900/(q+9)]·q = 900q/(q+9).
  2. This is a quotient, so: hi=900q, hi′=900. lo=q+9, lo′=1.
  3. Quotient rule: dr/dq = [(q+9)(900) − 900q(1)] / (q+9)².
  4. Expand the top: 900q+8100−900q = 8100 — the 900q pieces cancel out completely.
dr/dq = 8100 / (q+9)²

Example 46, part 2: evaluating dr/dq, then building dq/dm

First, find the actual q-value at m=9
  1. q = 10m²/√(m²+19). At m=9: q = 10(81)/√(81+19) = 810/√100 = 810/10.
q = 81, when m=9
Now plug that q into part 1's dr/dq
  1. dr/dq at q=81: 8100/(81+9)² = 8100/90² = 8100/8100.
dr/dq = 1, when m=9 (i.e. q=81)
Next: differentiate q with respect to m (quotient rule + chain rule, since √(m²+19) has its own inside)
  1. hi=10m², hi′=20m. lo=(m²+19)1/2, and by the chain rule lo′ = (1/2)(m²+19)−1/2(2m) = m/√(m²+19).
  2. Quotient rule: dq/dm = [lo·hi′ − hi·lo′] / lo² = [(m²+19)1/2(20m) − 10m²·m/√(m²+19)] / (m²+19).

This looks intimidating mainly because it's three rules layered together (quotient rule holding a chain rule holding a power rule) — but every individual piece is one we've already used earlier this week.

Example 46, part 3: finishing dq/dm, then the final answer

Plug m=9 into last page's dq/dm formula
  1. m²+19 = 81+19 = 100, so √(m²+19)=10.
  2. First piece: (m²+19)1/2(20m) = 10·20·9 = 1800.
  3. Second piece: 10m²·m/√(m²+19) = 10(81)(9)/10 = 729.
  4. Numerator: 1800−729 = 1071. Denominator: m²+19 = 100.
  5. dq/dm = 1071/100.
dq/dm = 10.71, when m=9
Finally — multiply the two pieces together (the chain rule from page 24)
  1. dr/dm = (dr/dq)·(dq/dm) = (1)(10.71).
dr/dm at m=9 is 10.71

In plain words: this manufacturer currently has 9 employees. Hiring a 10th one would bring in approximately an extra $10.71 per day in revenue. That's the entire payoff of this long problem — one dollar figure, telling the manufacturer whether hiring is worth it.
